\section{Motivation}
New Interfaces for Musical Expression (NIMEs) are an important field of research for making musical interfaces more accessible, expressive, creative, and fun. In real-time musical performance, NIMEs provide new ways for musicians to interact with digital systems, expanding the possibilities for improvisation, collaboration, and musical exploration. Music is a fundamental aspect of human culture and self-expression, and improving the ways people can interact with it can increase inclusion and participation in this important aspect of human culture. Additionally, as AI-driven creative tools become more sophisticated, they have the potential to redefine artistic collaboration between humans and machines, opening new pathways for emotional expression. By improving the expressiveness of NIMEs, we can not only enhance artistic possibilities but also contribute to a deeper, more accessible, and more human-centered approach to digital music interaction. By augmenting or transforming traditional instruments, or even introducing entirely new modes of interaction, NIMEs allow performers to push the boundaries of human-computer interaction in music. Real-time NIMEs enable dynamic and responsive musical systems that can interact with human performers in expressive ways, making them particularly valuable in live performance contexts. However, designing a system that can both understand and contribute meaningfully to a live musical performance remains a challenging task, requiring a balance between structured musical coherence and the flexibility of creative improvisation. This is a problem in developing interactive musical prediction systems (IMPS) which can continue a human performance in a coherent and expressive manner, and is the focus of our research. 

Recent developments in new interfaces for musical expression (NIMEs) which can be applied in real-time musical performance have broadly fallen into two categories. Heuristic based approaches break down musical harmony and structure symbolically into discrete components which can be logically reasoned with. By converting the expression of a human performer of the instrument into this symbolic language, a NIME can then dynamically interact with the performer by performing calculations in this logical space (TODO: include citation for a paper like this?). This includes rule-based methods, where hardcoded musical constraints direct composition, and search based techniques, which explore the space of future musical directions, while being guided by heuristics. These methods are excellent for generating structured, coherent, and theoretically sensical musical outputs. However, they suffer from restrictions on the breadth and complexity of musical expression, which often leads to robotic and less creative sounding results (TODO: need citation).

In contrast, other methods have used recurrent neural networks (RNNs) trained on human performance on an instrument to repeatedly predict the most likely next step in a performance. When used for NIMEs, these techniques solve many of the issues of heuristic-based approaches, as they are formulated to continuously predict the most human sounding continuation of a musical performance. However, these approaches aim for only the most likely next step in a performance, often leading to human-like, but very predictable and repetitive musical outputs. These is usually mitigated by adding randomness to the prediction process, by way of increased temperature, softmax sampling, or continuous sampling using Mixture Density Networks (MDNs). While adding randomness to the prediction process can make the performance more creative, it is a very arbitrary way to add this creativity and diversity, and has the tendancy to very quickly lead to incoherence if the randomness is too high.

This motivates the question of whether instead of adding randomness to the prediction process, a hybrid algorithm using both heuristic and RNN based approaches could be developed, with a search algorithm being used to guide the choice of RNN output from a function of output likelihoods within a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM). This would allow for the human-like performance of RNN based approaches, while also providing a framework for adding creativity and diversity without the sacrifice of coherence, by guiding outputs toward coherence with a set of structural parameters.

Within our research, we narrow the scope of this question to focus on the development of a new prediction algorithm for IMPSY (cite), an interactive musical prediction system which uses a Mixture Density Recurrent Neural Network (MDRNN) to continue human improvisation in a call-and-response manner, aiming to generate improvisation which coherently carries on from the human performer. Currently, IMPSY randomly samples from a GMM output at each step to mitigate the predictability of the RNN. In this thesis we develop a novel algorithm which uses Continuous Monte Carlo Tree Search (C-MCTS) to guide the choice of output from the GMM, measuring certain structural parameters of the music while the human performer plays, including their change over time, and then using the predicted values of these parameters as a heuristic to guide the choice of output from the GMM. This should allow for the system to generate more creative and diverse outputs, while enhancing the imitation ability and coherence of the system instead of detracting from it.

This current state of the art in intelligent musical instruments presents multiple important research questions which this paper aims to answer. Firstly, how can the strengths of symbolic approaches and RNN-based methods in NIMEs be combined into a single system which to achieve both short-term creativity and long-term structural coherence in real-time musical performances? Secondly, in terms of implementation, what are the most effective structural parameters for creating long-term coherence, and how can these be quantified and combined into a heuristic? Thirdly, how would a combined prediction system compare to existing approaches in terms of musical quality, structural coherence, and real-time performance capabilities, as assessed by human performers? By addressing these questions, we aim to develop a new framework for interactive musical prediction systems (IMPS) which combine both symbolic and neural network based approaches, to create better NIMEs as assessed by human performers.

Our combined approach for developing real-time interactive NIMEs uses an RNN based IMPS as a baseline algorithm, and extends it with a symbolic structural heuristic. As a baseline algorithm we use the mixture density recurrent neural network (MDRNN) IMPS developed by (cite An Interactive Musical Prediction System with Mixture Density Recurrent Neural Networks), which uses an RNN based approach. We then modify the prediction system. At each point where a prediction must be made, instead of outputting the first prediction made by the RNN, the RNN is run multiple times recursively, generating an expanding tree of possible branches the performance could continue along while sounding human like. On each of these branches, we then measure a set of structural parameters. Examples of these parameters are rhythmic uniformity, harmonic consonance, and pitch variation. Branches which are more consistent with the previous state of these structural parameters are given higher heuristic values, leading the choice of next prediction to favour musical directions which don't break from the long term structural norm in an incoherent way. When interacting with a human performer, these structural parameters, and the rate of change of each, will be tracked and used to inform the AI when it takes over from the human in the performance, ensuring that it continues the performance in the same structural direction that the human performer was moving in. This also allows for sudden breaks from structural parameters in places where it is deemed to be a reasonable direction for the piece, for example the beginning of a bridge section. We also present an interface for the human performer to control the structural parameters of the AI while it plays in a live setting, and for new and unique structural parameters to be trained from human performance, allowing for longer term parameters which are difficult to symbolically code, such as the mood of a piece, to be encoded using machine learning instead.

Using RNN predictions to determine on a short time scale which musical directions are appropriate ensures that the performance will sound more human and creative than symbolic approaches. The addition of symbolically encoded structural parameters and a tree search based system of choosing the musical direction of a piece negates the disadvantages of RNN based approaches, as it provides a framework for coherent long term structure, and balances out the greedy next step prediction process of a purely RNN based approach.
