\chapter{Evaluation}\label{chap:evaluation}
Within NIME research, what constitutes and evaluation is much more broad than in many other fields.~\cite{Barbosa2015EvaluationMeaning} discusses this breadth, noting that there are not only many aspects within prediction systems that can be evaluated, but many perspectives from which to evaluate them from, many goals the evaluation could target, many potential criteria the NIME could aim to meet, and many approaches to employ. Their paper covers these aspects, and notes that all of these aspects should be both considered and explicitly addressed during an evaluation in order to ensure that it is both comprehensive and clear. Hence, we will first cover these aspects and how they informed the design of our evaluations, and then move on to results themselves.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Target aspect:} The main aspect of the system we are evaluating is the ouput. The input, mapping, training, and methods of user interaction within the system have remained relatively unchanged from the prior IMPSY system~\citep{Martin2019IMPSY}, and so we will not be evaluating them. Given that our change in prediction algorithm creates a relevant distinction from IMPSY when it changes the output of the system, it makes sense for this to be our focus.
    \item \textbf{Perspective:} We evaluate from the perspective of the designer and the performer, as a user of our system will be both a performer and a designer, given the level of customisation available in the system. From a designers perspective, we evaluate the key technical metric our system aims to improve, the prediction capability. From the performers perspective, we evaluate the system in a qualitative trial, where improvisations are performed with the system and then analysed.
    \item \textbf{Goal:} Our main goal is the evaluate the quality of system output compared to the previous IMPSY system.
    \item \textbf{Criteria:} The criteria we will use also vary by section. First we evaluate the accuracy and speed of predictions, and then the playability and expressiveness of the system.
    \item \textbf{Approach:} Prediction accuracy and speed are evaluated quanitatively, whereas the playability and expressiveness of the system are evaluated qualitatively.
\end{itemize}

\section{Prediction Performance}
Prediction accuracy is the only relevant objective quantitative metric for evaluating the system. Given that the system is an interactive musical prediction system, its prediction accuracy is essential to its success. If the system is not able to predict the next note in a piece of music with any accuracy, then it will not be able to create a coherent improvisation.

\subsection{Datasets}
%TODO: Add citation information for the datasets according to Charles' recommendation. Include the proper format for citing datasets with DOIs if applicable, or provide a reference to the original source of the Nottingham Folk Music Database.

Two datasets were used to evaluate the system, the first is a dataset of 15 improvisations performed by the first author of this thesis. IMPSY is intended to function based on small models trained on personalised datasets of improvisations tuned to a sepcific performers style, and so this dataset is important for evaluating the system on one of its core intended use cases. The 15 improvisations contain a total of 11142 notes, and were performed as monophonic keyboard improvisations with the details of the model in mind. Cross-validation was used for all evaluations of this dataset, creating 15 models, each trained on 14 improvisations and evaluated on the remaining improvisation. This ensures that the model does not do well on evaluation metrics by simply overfitting to training data, and each improvisation had slightly different styles, tempos, keys and improvisational goals in order to further limit the possibility of overfitting.

For the second dataset, we used the Nottingham Folk Music Database. While this dataset does not contain compositions originally made monophonically, it does split each piece into a clearly defined chords and a monophonic melody, and so it is a good dataset to evaluate the system on. We take only the melody part of each piece, for a total of 1034 pieces and 196961 notes, and convert them into log files containing only the two dimensions of note duration and pitch for training. This dataset is also udeful as it is all within a similar style, and so is good for stylistic evaluation. From these 28 random melodies of at least 200 notes were selected as a test set, with a total of 11237 notes. The rest of the dataset was used to train a single model. A larger model size was used for this dataset, which is another benefit of using this second dataset, as it allows us to test whether our system improves performance even for models trained on larger datasets. A notable limitation of this dataset is that it does not contain tempo information, and so random tempos are assigned to each piece.

When evaluating a particular test piece from one of these datasets, the model takes the first 45 notes as its initial output memory, and then predicts the 46th note. Then the first note is dropped from memory, the actual 46th note is added, and the model predicts the 47th note. This process continues until the end of the test set, at which point it moves on to the next until all test sets have been evaluated. A prediction is considered correct if the exact correct pitch is predicted and the note duration is within an 8\% margin of error from the exact correct value. This 8\% figure was established through experiments with the tempo and swing heuristic, as from testing an average deviation of 8\% seemed to be the limit for where a tempo was clearly perceptable. Hence, it made sense to also use this as the cutoff figure for duration deviation in the evaluation. This may provide a slight bias in favour of the tempo and swing heuristics performance, as the evaluation metric matches how the heuristic was setup, but given that it was established through repeatable and logical testing and not a purposeful intention to bias, it is firstly not a major concern, and secondly shouldn't bias results very much from if independent tests were used to arrive at these two figures. The number of correctly predicted notes is tracked, and divided by the total number of predicted notes to give a prediction accuracy percentage.

To understand where the overall performance of the system comes from, it is important to evaluate how each heuristic component of the system performs in isolation, and also how it contributes to the overall performance of the system. We also break down the prediction accuracy metric into its two components, pitch and duration, to gain a better understanding of how the system performs in each of these areas, and the contribution of each component heuristic. In this subsection all predictions are conducted with a 100ms search time limit, to imitate a typical amount of search time which may be available in a real-time performance setting. All component heuristic evaluations are compared to two baselines, the predictive accuracy of the unguided MDRNN, representing the performance of the original IMPSY system without MCTS search, and the combined heuristic, representing the performance of our system with all heuristics combined and weighted. It is important to note that the two datasets provide quite different scales of accuracy, with accuracy being much higher for the Nottingham dataset. This is likely due to the fact that the Nottingham dataset is a larger and more structured dataset, while the improvisation dataset is smaller and more freeform. The Nottingham dataset also contains significantly more repetition than the improvisation dataset. These differences make the variation in performance for the improvisation dataset much more difficult to see on a combined graph, and the graph also becomes more cluttered. Hence, separate figures are provided for each dataset for all evaluations, and for clarity at a glance, improvisation dataset figures are shown in teal whereas nottingham dataset figures are hown in purple.

\subsubsection{Pitch Prediction Accuracy}

Firstly, individual component heuristic performance for pitch accuracy is evaluated for the improvisation dataset in figure~\ref{fig:individual_pitch_improv} and for the nottingham dataset in figure~\ref{fig:individual_pitch_nottingham}. Pitch accuracy is calculated by judging a prediction as correct if the predicted pitch is correct, regardless of the predicted time interval.

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/eval/individual_pitch_improv.png}
    \caption{Pitch prediction accuracy of each component heuristic for the improvisation dataset.\label{fig:individual_pitch_improv}}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/eval/individual_pitch_nottingham.png}
    \caption{Pitch prediction accuracy of each component heuristic for the nottingham dataset.\label{fig:individual_pitch_nottingham}}
\end{figure}

The key and modal and pitch interval heuristics unsurprisingly performed the best for pitch prediction, as they both focus on pitch. The repetition heuristic also performed well since it incorporates pitch information. As expected, the key and modal heuristic and the repetition heuristic performed significantly better on the nottingham dataset, where repetition and consistent keys were much more common, than they did on the improvisation dataset. The pitch interval heuristic performed strongly on both datasets, as it is not dependent on clear structural features. For the tempo and swing, and the time multiple heuristics, the performance was similar to unguided prediction in the nottingham dataset, and notably lower for the improvisation dataset. While it might be expected that these heuristics should perform very similarly to unguided prediction, given that they do not focus on pitch, a lowered prediction accuracy is not unexpected. This is because heuristic guidance which is not relevant to prediction accuracy only serves to dilute the relative probabilities of note options already provided by the MDRNN. While the MDRNNs most likely prediction could be the correct pitch, if a pitch agnostic heuristic is used, it will bias the search toward potential options with more likely time intervals, regardless of whether these options have significantly less likely pitch options. This is a key point to note, as it displays how heuristics must have a clear actively positive impact on prediction accuracy to provide a benefit to the system, as a neutral heuristic will have a negative impact by diluting the probability of the MDRNNs most likely options. Hence, the prediction accuracy not decreasing for the time interval focused heuristics in the nottingham dataset is the more surprising result. This could be explained by stronger correlation of rhythm and pitch, more regular rhyhtmic patterns, or some other factor or combination of factors in the nottingham dataset.

\subsubsection{Duration Prediction Accuracy}

Next, the duration prediction accuracy of each component heuristic is evaluated for the improvisation dataset in figure~\ref{fig:individual_time_improv} and for the nottingham dataset in figure~\ref{fig:individual_time_nottingham}. Duration accuracy is calculated by judging a prediction as correct if the predicted time interval is within 8\% of the correct value, regardless of the predicted pitch.

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/eval/individual_time_improv.png}
    \caption{Duration prediction accuracy of each component heuristic for the improvisation dataset.\label{fig:individual_time_improv}}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/eval/individual_time_nottingham.png}
    \caption{Duration prediction accuracy of each component heuristic for the nottingham dataset.\label{fig:individual_time_nottingham}}
\end{figure}

Once again, the relative performance of the heuristics in unsurprising. The tempo and swing heuristic and time multiple heuristic performed the best, as they are both focused on time intervals, with the time multiple heuristic performing well for both datasets, and the tempo and swing heuristic performing significantly better on the more structured nottingham dataset, with barely any effect on the improvisation dataset where tempo was much less consistent. Interestingly, the negative effect of neutral heuristics was significantly more pronounced for the duration prediction accuracy than it was for the pitch prediction accuracy. This effect was so strong that it brought the repetition heuristic back to being level with the unguided MDRNN for both datasets, and for the improvisation dataset it led to the combined heuristic underperforming unguided MDRNN predictions. It is hard to know exactly why this is the case, but a potential explanation is that rhythm is more foundational to the structure of a melody than pith is. This means that when predicting pitch, the underlying rhythmic structure still provides valuable context and a strong basis for the prediction, even if the heuristic is not directly relevant to pitch. However, when predicting duration, pitch information provides significantly less relevant context for the underlying rhythmic structure, and so pitch focused heuristics have a much more pure dilutive effective on duration prediction accuracy, whereas choices about rhythm are unlikely to dilute the most likely pitch options.

\subsubsection{Total Prediction Accuracy}

The final evaluation of the individual component heuristics is the total prediction accuracy, which is calculated by judging a prediction as correct if both the predicted pitch and time interval are correct. This is evaluated for the improvisation dataset in figure~\ref{fig:individual_total_improv} and for the nottingham dataset in figure~\ref{fig:individual_total_nottingham}.

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/eval/individual_total_improv.png}
    \caption{Total prediction accuracy of each component heuristic for the improvisation dataset.\label{fig:individual_total_improv}}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/eval/individual_total_nottingham.png}
    \caption{Total prediction accuracy of each component heuristic for the nottingham dataset.\label{fig:individual_total_nottingham}}
\end{figure}

The total prediction accuracy is the most relevant metric for evaluating the system, as it is the only metric which directly measures the systems ability to predict the next note. Ideally, all component heuristics should perform above unguided predictions for this metric. The results look, as expected, part way inbetween their pitch and duration prediction results relative to the unguided MDRNN. The pitch interval markov model heuristic had the strongest performance for the improvisation dataset, as its pitch prediction accuracy was by far the highest, and its negative effect on duration prediction accuracy was not enough to reduce its overall performance significantly. The repetition heuristic performed the best for the nottingham dataset, due to its very strong pitch prediction accuracy, and neutral effect on duration prediction accuracy. Interestingly, the pitch interval markov model heuristic had the weakest performance of any heuristic for the nottingham dataset, despite being the strongest for the improvisation dataset. This displays how different datasets with different structures, styles, and sizes, can have a significant impact on which heuristics perform best. The only heuristic to underperform unguided predictions was the key and modal heuristic for the improvisation dataset, as it appears that its signficant negative effect on duration prediction accuracy was not outweighed by its positive effect on pitch prediction accuracy. However, this does not mean that it is not a useful component of the combined heuristic, as it could be that the inclusion of other heuristics help to cancel out its negative effects while allowing its positive effects to remain.

\subsubsection{Contribution Assessment}

This line of examination leads us to the next evaluation, which is the performance of the combined heuristic when each component heuristic is excluded. This is evaluated for the improvisation dataset in figure~\ref{fig:combined_total_improv} and for the nottingham dataset in figure~\ref{fig:combined_total_nottingham}. The combined heuristic is given its standard heuristic component weights, but with the excluded heuristic given a weight of 0. This shows how much the overall performance of the combined heuristic is affected by each component heuristic, and whether the combination of heuristics makes some component heuristics redundant, or whether they help to balance each others weaknesses.

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/eval/combined_total_improv.png}
    \caption{Total prediction accuracy of the combined heuristic when excluding each component heuristic for the improvisation dataset.\label{fig:combined_total_improv}}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/eval/combined_total_nottingham.png}
    \caption{Total prediction accuracy of the combined heuristic when excluding each component heuristic for the nottingham dataset.\label{fig:combined_total_nottingham}}
\end{figure}

It is critical that the exclusion of any given component heuristic leads to a notable drop in performance for at least one of the datasets, and at a minimum doesn't increase performance for the other dataset. Fortunately, for both datasets the exclusion of any component heuristic results in a drop in performance. And the exclusion of each component heuristic leads to a significant drop in performance for at least one of the datasets. This shows that none of the component heuristics are redundant, and that they all provide a unique contribution to the overall performance of the system. It is also important to note that the key and modal heuristic, which had a negative effect of prediction accuracy on the improvisation dataset when used in isolation, caused a notable drop in performance when removed from the combined heuristic. This shows that the inclusion of the other heuristic components helped to cancel out its negative effects on duration prediction accuracy, and allowed it to have an impact mostly when it was relevant for pitch prediction. Another notable result is the two heuristic components which had relatively neutral effects on prediction accuracy when removed from the combined heuristic. This was the repetition heuristic for the improvisation dataset, likely because there is very little repetition in the improvisation dataset, and so it is made redudant by the pitch interval and time multiple heuristics, which it likely partly replaced the function of when used individually to generate its small positive effect on prediction accuracy. In contrast, for the nottingham dataset the loss of the repetition heuristic caused the largest drop in performance, whereas the loss of the time multiple heuristic caused very little. In this dataset repetition is much more common, and so the repetition heuristic is able to provide a much larger positive effect on prediction accuracy, whereas note duration is much more consistent and predictable, and so the MDRNN is likely able to predict it quite well. For individual performance, the time multiple heuristic likely gained most of its performance through partially replacing the function of the repetition heuristic, and so in the combined heuristic it is made relatively redudant.

\subsection{Performance Scaling of the Combined Heuristic}
Evaluation of the combined heuristic has so far been limited to lines of each previous figure, providing a point of comparison for judging the performance of each component heuristic. However, it is also important to evaluate and display the performance of the combined heuristic in more detail. This performance is measured relative to the important metrics of number of search iterations and search time. The number of search iterations is the number of simulations conducted in the MCTS search, and given that more simulations leads to a larger search tree and more rigorous search, it is the main parameter impacting the performance of the system. However, increasing the number of search iterations also increases the search time, and so it is important to evaluate the trade-off between these two parameters.

Firstly, the total prediction accuracy of the combined heuristic is evaluated for the improvisation dataset in figure~\ref{fig:improv_nodes_to_accuracy} and for the nottingham dataset in figure~\ref{fig:nottingham_nodes_to_accuracy} relative to the number of MCTS iterations on a log scale. Data for $10^n$ iterations with $n$ increasing by increments of 0.25 from 0 to 4 are shown, with an asymptotic fit calculated by non-linear regression.

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/eval/improv_nodes_to_accuracy.png}
    \caption{Total prediction accuracy of the combined heuristic for the improvisation dataset by number of MCTS iterations (log scale).\label{fig:improv_nodes_to_accuracy}}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/eval/nottingham_nodes_to_accuracy.png}
    \caption{Total prediction accuracy of the combined heuristic for the nottingham dataset by number of MCTS iterations (log scale).\label{fig:nottingham_nodes_to_accuracy}}

The first important point to note is that significant performance improvements are seen for both datasets with even a very small number of MCTS iterations. This is a very positive result, and demonstrates the effectiveness of our system even on slow hardware and with short search times. Given that one of the main limitations of our system is the significant increase to prediction time when using a form of MCTS search which requires many MDRNN outputs for a single prediction, as opposed to one output for the previous method, it is promising to see the immediate benefit of small numbers of MCTS iterations. However, the limitations of this slow form of MCTS search are still clear. The continuing improvements in performance with more iterations are clerly shown by the asymptotic fits, which show that at 1000 iterations the system still has a significant amount of estimated room for improvement. It is important to note that the asymptotic maximums estimated by the fits are very loose estimations and cannot be relied on to be accurate without much more data at higher amounts of iterations, but they do provide a useful educated guess at the maximum potential performance of the system. Another useful point to note is that the asymptotic fits for the nottingham dataset begin to level out at a lower number of iterations than for the improvisation dataset. This is likely for the same reasons that the nottingham dataset sees larger prediction accuracy improvements in general, being that because it is a dataset with more structured and consistent pieces, the structurally focused heuristics in our system are able to provide a larger benefit, and can establish this benefit with a smaller search tree. \section{Qualitative Performance Experience}
While prediction accuracy is a very important metric, and one which is much easier to rigorously evaluate than others due to being objective and quantifiable, it is not the only metric which is important for evaluating the system.~\cite{Wagstaff2012MlMatters} notes that accuracy only measures a systems ability to recreate data in the style of the dataset it was trained on, but this may not always be the desired result. If a system produces an output which is not the correct prediction, but through increased creativity is a prediction which a performer views more highly than the `correct' prediction, then this should be considered a success. During its practical use, our system is being viewed from the perspective of the performer, not the designer, and so evaluating our system from this perspective is important. Many of our modifications to IMPSY were made to help it conform to important structural musical parameters of a piece, and this could potentially lead to more rigid and less interesting improvisations despite the increase in prediction accuracy, a potential limitation which requires testing. The system is also designed to be interactive, acting in a call and response manner with the performer, and this aspect of the system has not yet been evaluated. This type of evaluation also allows for qualitative perspectives on the outputs to be shared, which, given the level of subjectivity and nuance in music, provide important commentary that quantiative methods cannot provide. 

To evaluate the system from the perspective of a performer, a series of improvisations has been conducted with our system, and will be subjectively and qualitatively analysed on thieir musical qualities. For this section of the evaluation, I will act as the performer, and only the model trained on my own improvisations will be used, imitating a practical use of the system. I have performed and recorded 4 call and response improvisations with the system, each lasting slightly over 5 minutes. The first pair of improvisations tested a structured performance, with clear key signatures and tempos that were adhered to in specific sections. The first of these was a \href{https://youtu.be/0DQhZ1FaKT4}{structured improvisation with our new MCTS prediction system} and the second was a \href{https://youtu.be/6BZlfXHocU8}{structured improvisation using the old MDRNN only prediction system}. The goal of these structured improvisations was to test the musical coherence and playability of the system, to see how well it was able to remain within the structure of the piece, and respond to the performers input in an appropriate manner. The second pair of improvisations tested a more freeform performance, with drifting time signatures and tempos. The first of these was a \href{https://youtu.be/2op_NtcvT8M}{freeform improvisation with our new MCTS prediction system} and the second was a \href{https://youtu.be/8ncMNEG4Ang}{freeform improvisation using the old MDRNN only prediction system}. The goal of these freeform improvisations was to test the expressiveness and creativity of the system, to see how well it was able to generate interesting and unique musical ideas, and whether it could still maintain coherent call and response interactions with the performer when the performer was not adhering to a strict structure. Section conclusion:
From qualitative analysis of the system from a performers perspective, it appears that the prediction accuracy improvements our system provides are not at the cost of playability or expressiveness. During more structured improvisations, the use of MCTS significantly improved musical coherence, playability, and even expressiveness when compared to the previous MDRNN only version of IMPSY. The system was able to maintain a coherent call and response interaction with the performer, and was able to generate interesting and unique musical ideas. During more freeform improvisations, the difference in performance was less clear, and the MCTS system provided weaker outputs than during structured improvisations. This is unsurprising, as multiple of the component heuristics in our system are defined with specific structures like tempo and key signature in mind, and so when these structures are not present these components deactivate and cannot provide any benefit. However, even in these freeform improvisations, our system generated more coherent outputs than the pure MDRNN method, and this made it more easy to continue on from in call and response. This did not come at the expense of expressiveness and creativity either, as improvements in prediction accuracy seem from qualitative analysis to be mostly based on improving musical coherence of otherwise incoherent predictions, which allows for more interesting musical ideas to be generated which don't immediately dissolve into incoherence. Overall, the system appears to be a significant improvement over the previous IMPSY system, and does not have any obvious qualitative weaknesses in comparison.\
