\chapter{Evaluation}\label{chap:evaluation}
Within NIME research, what constitutes and evaluation is much more broad than in many other fields.~\cite{Barbosa2015EvaluationMeaning} discusses this breadth, noting that there are not only many aspects within prediction systems that can be evaluated, but many perspectives from which to evaluate them from, many goals the evaluation could target, many potential criteria the NIME could aim to meet, and many approaches to employ. Their paper covers these aspects, and notes that all of these aspects should be both considered and explicitly addressed during an evaluation in order to ensure that it is both comprehensive and clear. Hence, we will first cover these aspects and how they informed the design of our evaluations, and then move on to results themselves.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Target aspect:} The main aspect of the system we are evaluating is the ouput. The input, mapping, training, and methods of user interaction within the system have remained relatively unchanged from the prior IMPSY system~\citep{Martin2019IMPSY}, and so we will not be evaluating them. Given that our change in prediction algorithm creates a relevant distinction from IMPSY when it changes the output of the system, it makes sense for this to be our focus.
    \item \textbf{Perspective:} The perspectives it is possible to view our system from are the designer, the audience, and the performer. All of these perspectives are relevant to our evaluation, and so we will be evaluating all three in different sections. From a designers perspective, we evaluate the key technical metric our system aims to improve, the prediction capability. From the audiance perspective, we assess the quality of the generated predictions in a blind test. And from the performers perspective, we evaluate the system in a qualitative trial, where improvisations are performed with the system and then analysed.
    \item \textbf{Goal:} Our main goal is the evaluate the quality of system output compared to the previous IMPSY system.
    \item \textbf{Criteria:} The criteria we will use also vary by section. First we evaluate the accuracy and speed of predictions; then the perceived quality, cohesion, and style of the output; and finally the playability and expressiveness of the system.
    \item \textbf{Approach:} Prediction accuracy, quality, and cohesion are all evaluated quanitatively, although note that the criteria for prediction accuracy is objective, while the criteria for quality and cohesion are subjective. The playability and expressiveness of the system are evaluated qualitatively.
\end{itemize}

\section{Prediction Performance}
Prediction accuracy is the only relevant objective quantitative metric for evaluating the system. Given that the system is an interactive musical prediction system, its prediction accuracy is essential to its success. If the system is not able to predict the next note in a piece of music with any accuracy, then it will not be able to create a coherent improvisation.

\subsection{Datasets}

Two datasets were used to evaluate the system, the first is a dataset of 15 improvisations performed by the first author of this thesis. IMPSY is intended to function based on small models trained on personalised datasets of improvisations tuned to a sepcific performers style, and so this dataset is important for evaluating the system on one of its core intended use cases. The 15 improvisations contain a total of 11142 notes, and were performed as monophonic keyboard improvisations with the details of the model in mind. Each of these improvisations is used as a separate testing set, in each case with a model trained on the other 14 improvisations being used. This allows all notes to be part of the evaluation set.

For the second dataset, we used the Nottingham Folk Music Database. While this dataset does not contain compositions originally made monophonically, it does split each piece into a clearly defined chords and a monophonic melody, and so it is a good dataset to evaluate the system on. We take only the melody part of each piece, for a total of 1034 pieces and 196961 notes, and convert them into log files containing only the two dimensions of note duration and pitch for training. This dataset is also udeful as it is all within a similar style, and so is good for stylistic evaluation. From these 28 random melodies of at least 200 notes were selected as a test set, with a total of 11237 notes. The rest of the dataset was used to train a single model. A larger model size was used for this dataset, which is another benefit of using this second dataset, as it allows us to test whether our system improves performance even for models trained on larger datasets. A notable limitation of this dataset is that it does not contain tempo information, and so random tempos are assigned to each piece.

\section{Subjective Perception of Output Quality}
While prediction accuracy is a very important metric, and one which is much easier to test than others due to it's objective and quantitative nature, it cannot be used as a complete evaluation of the system.~\cite{Wagstaff2012MlMatters} notes that accuracy only measures a systems ability to recreate data from the dataset it was trained on, but this may not always be the desired result. If a system produces an output which is not the correct prediction, but remains a creative and interesting prediction which can produce more pleasing outcomes for an audience or performer, then this should be considered a success. In practical use, a system is not being viewed from the perspective of its designer, but by its audience and performer, and so evaluating our system from these perspectives is also important. Many of our modifications to the system were made to help it conform to the important strcutural parameters of the piece, and this could potentially lead to more rigid and less interesting improvisations despite the increase in prediction accuracy. 

In this section, we specifically look at the audiences perspective. A user study could not practically be included within this thesis, and so the first author of this thesis is a proxy for the audience, and will be referred to as the audience for the remainder of this section. To test audience experience, all 15 improvisations from the first dataset and 28 evaluation pieces from the second dataset are used. Each is played to a random point, and then the system takes over for a following 30 seconds. For all test cases both our system and the original IMPSY system are used, and the audience rates the quality of the improvisation on a scale of 1-10, with 1 being extremely poor and 10 being on the level of a strong human improvisation. Cohesiveness is also rated on a scale of 1-10, with 1 being as incoherent as randomly generated notes and 10 being an improvisation with a strong sense of structure and flow which follows sensically from the previous context of the piece. The audience finally rates the system on a scale of 1-10 for how well it follows the performers style, with 1 being completely different and 10 being almost indistinguishable from the performer. \section{Performance Experience}

The system is designed to be used by a performer, and so it is important to evaluate how well it works from their perspective. The system is also designed to be interactive, acting in a call and response manner with the performer, and this aspect of the system has not yet been evaluated. This type of evaluation also allows for qualitative perspectives on the outputs to be shared, which, given the level of subjectivity and nuance in music, provide important commentary that quantiative methods cannot provide.
