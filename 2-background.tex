\chapter{Background}\label{chap:background}
This thesis relates to work in multiple different fields, including musical controllers, generative music, and robot motion planning, and this chapter provides an overview of each of these fields, connecting back to their relevance to our research. It will cover the advantages and disadvantages of various AI methods and their applications to computer music, and discuss gaps in research which we aim to cover within this thesis. This chapter also covers the machine learning and search algorithms used in our system design, their previous applications, and their potential to be applied within our research. We begin with an overview of New Interfaces for Musical Expression (NIMEs), including a brief history of developments in computer music which led to the creation of the field, important NIME papers, and the major subclasses of NIMEs. We then discuss how machine learning has been used in music, and explore some different types of neural networks and their relevance to our research. This is followed by a look into heuristic based AI, a different approach than machine learning based techniques, and its advantages and disadvantages in music generation. Next, the background for search methods not commonly used in previous computer music literature, but which are used in our own system design, or in other fields where similar combinations of machine learning and search are utilised, is covered. We then discuss where combinations of machine learning and search which have crossover to our intended system design have already achieved impressive results. Finally, we briefly cover IMPSY, the system our research builds on, how it combines many computer music and machine learning techniques previously discussed to great effect, what its weaknesses and gaps in function are, and how applying combined machine learning and search techniques found in other fields can address some of these weaknesses.

\section{New Interfaces for Musical Expression (NIMEs)}
\textit{The Cambridge Companion to Electronic Music} by~\cite{Collins2012Cambridge} describes three eras of music programming. The first era began with MUSIC-1, the first digital computer music synthesis program, which was followed by a series of versions collectively referred to as MUSIC-N. MUSIC-N introduced the concept of algorithmic composition, and is the precursor to modern computer music. The second era was defined by the introduction of real-time music programming languages, such as Max~\citep{Puckette2002Max} and Pure Data~\citep{Puckette1997PD}. Pure data is a graphical drag and drop modular programming language which allows for algorithmic compositions to be edited during performance, by modifying the code of the system while a performance is in progress. While live performance with pure data is rudimentary compared to modern alternatives, it was a large step forward in the computer music space. The third and current era of music programming refers to the surge in more real-time and interactive musical interfaces, and a shift from programming being a tool to create musical algorithms, instruments, and performances, to programs becoming the instruments themselves. NIMEs are at the core of what this current era represents. The term NIME originally comes from a conference series of the same name, which began in 2001 and now annually showcases the latest NIME related research.

The key category of NIMEs we are interested in for our research are real-time interactive NIMEs. These are NIMEs where the user and system interact in real-time during musical performance, and can be approximately split into three main subcategories. The first is NIMEs focused on the mapping from a controller to a musical output, such as gesture based controllers, or augmented instruments. The second involves simultaneous collaborative performances involving independently acting users and systems, such as human-robot collaborative ensembles. Finally, the third subcategory is interactive musical prediction systems, where the system learns from the user and contributes accompaniment, including in the form of call and response. The call and response mode of interactive musical prediction systems is the main focus area of this thesis.

\subsection{Controller Mappings}
A type of controller which mandated innovation in mapping controller inputs to musical outputs is gesture-based controllers. Early gestural musical controllers such as the Radio Baton and Conductor Program helped to establish early frameworks for three-dimensional gestural control~\citep{Lawson1977Conductor,Mathews1991RadioBaton}. These advances were a step toward the use of this technology in commercial devices such as the Wii Remote and Microsoft Kinect, devices which have influenced gestural controllers in many fields including music, and made gestural technology accesible and recognisable to the broader public~\citep{Kiefer2008Wiimote,Zhang2012Kinect,Takase2021CameraMelodyLSTM}.

Other work focused on making mapping strategies which were generalisable to many types of input and output.~\cite{Hunt2000MappingStrategies} introduced a generalised mapping strategy which uses a two-stage rules-based concept which could allow for any input controller to control the same musical output function, this was done by the initial stage, which is controller specific, converting controller inputs into values of broad musical concepts, and a second stage converting those values into musical outputs. The Wekinator, created by~\cite{Fiebrink2009Wekinator} was even more generalisable, allowing performers to train custom machine learning models to map controller inputs to musical outputs through user provided training examples. Figure~\ref{fig:Wekinator} shows this interactive learning process, which is similar to the process used in IMPSY~\cite{Martin2019IMPSY}. More recent applications involve mapping strategies where the mapping itself can perform the vast majority of the music generation, while still providing a level of control to the user, such as text to music generation models~\citep{Wu2024MusicControlNet}.

Augmented traditional instruments represent another significant approach, and can add enhanced control capabilities to instruments, adapt their outputs, or both, without sacrificing their familiarity.~\cite{Machover1989Hyperinstruments} created the hyperinstruments project, which was an early work focusing on control capabilities by enhancing traditional instruments with sensors and digital processing capabilities. A more recent example is the Magnetic Resonator Piano by~\cite{McPherson2010AugmentedPiano}, which utilises electromagnetic actuators to directly manipulate acoustic piano strings, creating new sound possibilities while maintaining the acoustic piano as a base.

Our research aims to be agnostic to the type of controller, mapping strategy, and output used in a similar way to the Wekinator~\citep{Fiebrink2009Wekinator}, as any instrument which can map its controls to a set number of dimensions can be used to train a model for the system. This is particularly important for our research, as the system is designed to adapt to the unique control strategies of each performer, and it would be odd to enable this adaptation for only a single instrument.
 
\subsection{Collaborative Networked Instruments}
Collaborative networked instruments have extend the concept of NIMEs beyond individual performance, allowing multiple performers to interact simultaneously across physical and virtual spaces, and for AI and human performers to collaborate in simultaneous performance. While more primative collaborative networked performances had been conducted earlier, the spreading of high speed internet in the early 2000s is what led to it becoming a more commonly researched topic~\citep{Renaud2007NetworkedMusic}. Early research often focused simply on producing some kind of engaging performance which utilised a distributed computer archeticture, with the core difficulties being synchronization strategies and communication protocols, for example the Stanford Laptop Orchestra~\citep{Wang2009Slork} conducted many such performances. Other work has focused more on systems where perception of what the other performers are doing is the main difficulty, such as a system designed by~\cite{Weinberg2018MultiHumanRobot} where AI systems and human performers must interact and improvise in real-time to produce coherent musical output. While our research does not work with simultaneous collaborative systems, it does still require call-and-response collaboration between one human and one AI performer, and so this work on perceiving human playing style in order to respond coherently is a core part of our research. By tracking musical parameters important to the human perceptual framework, such as rhythmic stability~\citep{Desain2002RhythmicStability}, our system can match these parameters when responding to the human performer and hence better follow on from human improvisation. While these methods are very common in collaborative networked systems, they are not as common in the machine learning based interactive musical prediction systems we aim to improve upon, and so integrating these methods into our system could fill a gap in that field.

\subsection{Interactive Musical Prediction Systems}  
Interactive musical prediction systems anticipate and respond to performer actions based on learned patterns and musical knowledge. These systems include rule-based systems based on knowledge of musical theory, either learned or hardcoded; markov-based approaches, probability trees, and other types of probabilistic predictions of a next musical action; and various neural network architectures capable of learning to make musical predictions from scratch~\citep{Collins2007InteractiveMusic}. An example of a markov-based system is the Continuator by~\cite{Pachet2002Continuator}, which builds a database of recognised musical patterns from a training set of human improvisation, and from this database generates a markov model to generate predictions and continue from human performance in call-and-response fashion. These earlier types of call-and-response systems form the background for the development of IMPSY, the core system our research builds on~\citep{Martin2019IMPSY}. IMPSY uses a mixture density recurrent neural network (MDRNN) to allow for collaborative call and response performances between human performers and an AI system by predicting the most likely next intrument control values. IMPSY will be explored in more depth later in the chapter.

\section{Machine Learning in Music}
Machine learning techniques expand the possibilities of NIMEs significantly by allowing for sophisticated and human-like generative music. Unlike rule-based approaches which rely on hardcoding of musical knowledge, machine learning models can discover patterns and relationships directly from musical data, allowing for more adaptive musical interactions. A well-trained musical prediction model can anticipate likely continuations of musical phrases, suggest complementary harmonizations, or generate real-time accompaniments to human performance on a variety of instruments. NIMEs can also be generalisable to multiple models trainable by users, allowing for easy expansion to different instruments and performance styles. The creation of effective machine learning based NIMEs requires an ability to capture both local patterns and global structures inherent in music. They must also balance the advantages of purely exploitative strategies, which aim to consistently generate the most likely next note, with explorative ones, which aim for more unique and human-like results. Many different machine learning architectures have been explored in attempt to balance these objectives, and each offers their own advantages and disadvantages. This section examines five key approaches that have proven particularly valuable for musical prediction tasks.

\subsection{Markov Models}
Markov models have been widely used in algorithmic composition due to their ability to capture local musical patterns and transitions. These probabilistic models operate on the principle that future states depend only on the current state, as well as sometimes a finite history of states, making them particularly suitable for modeling sequential musical events where local context strongly influences prediction. They are incredibly efficient computationally, requiring only a transition matrix to represent the probabilities of moving between states. However, this simplicity comes at the cost of limited expressive power, as Markov models struggle to capture long-term dependencies and hierarchical structures present in music.

The most basic form of Markov model is a first-order Markov chain. These chains model transitions where the next state depends solely on the current state, represented by a simple transition probability matrix. While computationally efficient, they often produce musically simplistic results that cannot replicate long-term structure, as shown by~\cite{Zicarelli1987JamFactory} in Figure 13. In contrast, higher-order Markov chains incorporate longer histories by conditioning predictions on sequences of previous states, enabling more coherent musical phrases and motifs. Figure~\ref{fig:first-order-markov} shows a basic first-order Markov model of note transitions, and Figure~\ref{fig:first-order-markov} is a valid potential second-order version of this first order model. Note how the second order model is larger in size and more complex, but can also capture more detail, such as that the D$\rightarrow$A transition should only ever occur when the previous note was F\textsuperscript{$\sharp$}, and the note sequence A$\rightarrow$D$\rightarrow$A cannot occur.

Higher order markov chains, while more powerful, suffer from exponential growth as their order increases. This problem led to~\cite{Ron1996VOMM} developing the Variable-order Markov model (VOMM), which adaptively determines the appropriate context length for prediction based on available training data. This adaptability allows VOMMs to capture both short patterns when data is sparse and longer sequences when following significantly well travelling paths in the training data, making them particularly valuable for interactive systems where response time and predictive accuracy must be balanced.

Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) are another type of Markov Model where the states are only partially observable, and the goal of the system is to predict the most likely series of states which generate a visible set of transitions. For example,~\cite{Allan2004HMM} show how having chords as hidden states, but melody as a visible transition, can create a model which predicts viable chord accompaniments to melodies.~\cite{Simon2008MarkovAccompany} develops this concept further, allowing users with no musical experience to sing and have accompaniment automatically generated for them by a HMM. HMMs are particularly useful in these kinds of cases where there is a clear distinction between a known element, and an unknown element which must be generated based on the known element. 

Markov models effectively generate simple accompaniments, recognise common patterns in musical styles, create reasonable results with limited training data, and can easily be made computationally efficient enough for real-time applications. However, they are limited by their simplicity, as they can only capture local patterns and transitions, and struggle to model complex musical structures. This limitation has motivated the development of more sophisticated neural network architectures that can capture more complex and less well-defined aspects of music.

\subsection{Feedforward Neural Networks}
Feedforward neural networks (FNNs) are a step inbetween markov models and recurrent neural networks. Unlike Markov models, which function based on explicit transition probabilities, FNNs can discover non-linear relationships within musical data, which can capture more complex musical structures. However, FNNs face limitations in handling sequential data, as they require fixed-size inputs and lack mechanisms for capturing long-term dependencies. The name `feedforward' refers to the one directional flow of information through the network, where each layer's output serves as the input to the next layer, without any loops or backward flowing feedback connections. Figure~\ref{fig:feedforward} shows how a feedforward network could be designed for an augmented instrument mapping strategy, where the first layer is the input layer, which could encode the controls of the instrument, there are then many hidden layers which transform these controls in complex ways, and then finally an output layer which produces a sound. In this figure this example is a remapping of a keyboard to allow it to produce vibrato. 

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/feedforward.png}
    \caption{A hypothetical feedforward network mapping keyboard inputs to new set of outputs, including vibrato.\label{fig:feedforward}}
\end{figure}

While FNNs could theoretically be used for simple sequential musical functions, they are much better suited to musical classification tasks, such as evaluating rhythms~\citep{Dolson1989Feedforward}, chord classification~\citep{Laden1989ChordClassification}, and musical style classification~\citep{Dannenberg1997StyleClassification}. This is because their limitations made them unsuitable for sequential tasks such as musical prediction or generation. Their requirement of a fixed size input size means that musical sequences must be encoded into fixed length vectors, often forcing dimensionality reduction which leads to the loss of important information. Additionally, their lack of feedback connections means that they cannot remember previous inputs, making them unsuitable for temporal data like music. However, FNNs established important foundations for the later development and use of recurrent neural networks.

\subsection{Recurrent Neural Networks}
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) extend beyond the limitations of both Markov models and feedforward neural networks by maintaining internal states that can capture more complex temporal dependencies in musical sequences. Unlike FNNs, which process inputs independently with no inbuild mechanism for processing sequences, RNNs incorporate backward flowing feedback connections which allow information to persist across time steps~\citep{LeCun2015DeepLearningConcepts}. In addition, RNNs utilise parameter sharing, which allows them propogate learning weights within the network over multiple time steps, and hence not have to repeatedly relearn already determined musical rules which are likely to persist over time. However, RNNs can suffer from the vanishing and exploding gradient problems, where gradient information either diminishes or grows exponentially as it is propagated backward through time~\citep{Bengio1994RNNGradientProblems}. In practice, this means basic RNNs often fail to capture important long-range dependencies that create coherent musical phrases and sections.

RNN architecture consists of a hidden state that acts as a form of memory, allowing information to persist across time steps. This hidden state is updated at each time step based on both new input and its previous value, making RNNs particularly suited for sequence modeling tasks. Weights are kept when the hidden state is updated, allowing the network to learn long-term dependencies in the data. Figure~\ref{fig:rnn} shows a simple RNN architecture, where the input is fed into the network at each time step, and the hidden state is updated based on both the input and the previous hidden state. The output of the network is then generated from the hidden state, which can be used to predict the next note in a sequence. When successive input layers repeatedly increase or decrease network weights, their transfer to future time steps can lead to exploding/vanishing gradients in the hidden layers.

\begin{figure}[th!]
    \includegraphics[width=12cm]{figures/rnn.png}
    \caption{A simple RNN archeticture, where the input, hidden, and output layers are compressed representations of the layers shown in Figure~\ref{fig:feedforward}.\label{fig:rnn}}
\end{figure}

~\cite{Todd1989RNNComposition} was an early example of using RNNs for composition, and split music into a series of discrete 16th note blocks, which were then used to train a simple RNN to predict the next note in the sequence. The recurrence in the network allowed it to learn melodies up to 20 notes in length, and this helped it to generate melodies with longer dependencies than previous markov model approaches. Another example is the CONCERT system~\citep{Mozer1994CONCERT}, which includes pitch, duration, and chord as outputs. This removes the need for discrete 16th note blocks, as duration is now a continuous variable. It can also learn at the phrase level to generate higher level musical structures, and was shown to be both more concise and more general as a model than third order markov models.

RNNs established the foundation for neural sequence modeling in music generation systems and demonstrated the potential for learning-based approaches to capture musical relationships across multiple time scales. However, their architectural limitations, particularly in regards to gradient issues preventing long-term memory, ultimately motivated the development of more sophisticated recurrent architectures specifically designed to address the challenges of long-term dependency modeling.

\subsection{Long Short Term Memory Networks}
Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTMs), created by~\cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM}, are a type of RNN which addresses the vanishing and exploding gradient problems of standard RNNs, enabling more effective learning of long-term dependencies crucial for musical coherence. Their gated architecture provides selective memory capabilities that better align with musical structures spanning multiple time scales. LSTMs can remember critical information over extended periods, but also forget irrelevant information, keeping them computationally efficient and effective for music generation tasks. Most of the time that RNNs are mentioned in other sections of this thesis, we are referring to LSTMs, as these are the most commonly used RNNs in music generation tasks today~\citep{Ji2024DeepLearningInMusicSurvey}.

The LSTM architecture introduces three specialized gates; the input, forget, and output gates; which regulate information flow through the network. These gates determine which information should be added to the cell state, which should be removed, and which should be output, allowing the network to selectively preserve important features while discarding irrelevant ones. This selective memory mechanism is particularly valuable for music, where relationships between notes can span varying temporal distances depending on the musical context. Figure~\ref{fig:lstm} shows a diagram of the technical workings of the LSTM architecture. An understanding of each how each of these gates work and the functions used is not necessary for an understanding of this thesis.

    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/lstm.png}
    \caption{Reproduction of Figure 4 from~\cite{Khatri2023LSTMFigure}. The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) cell architecture.\label{fig:lstm}}
\end{figure}

In music generation, LSTMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities. Unlike standard RNNs, they can maintain coherent harmonic progressions across extended musical phrases while simultaneously managing multiple independent voices. The first notable use of LSTMs for music generation was by~\cite{Eck2002LSTMMusic}, which training an LSTM to generate blues improvisations. It had improved structural coherence over standard RNNs, although still not close to the structural coherence of human performance. A more modern LSTM prediction system is DeepBach~\citep{Hadjeres2017DeepBach}, which attempted to generate convincing chorale harmonizations in the style of Bach. The results showed that on average, around 50\% of listeners thought a given DeepBach harmonization was composed by Bach, compared to scores of around 80\% for Bach's actual compositions. This shows that LSTMs can produce results which are very close to human performance, and even fool expert listeners in some cases. However, this system still had issues with understanding relative lessons about music, as it was not translation invariant, and was still only operating with relatively short pieces in a very limited and rules-based style of music.~\cite{Johnson2017InvariantLSTMs} provided a solution to the former problem by developing a method to make translation invarient LSTMs, however, this method was only effective when trained on a single musical style, similarly to how DeepBach was, and training on multiple styles would lead to confusing stylistic shifts within a piece. The DeepJ model~\citep{Mao2018DeepJ} later solved this issue by adding specific style conditions at each layer of the network.

Despite their advantages over standard RNNs and multiple decades of improvements, LSTMs still face multiple limitations~\citep{Ji2024DeepLearningInMusicSurvey}. They still struggle with very long-term dependencies, such as musical motifs. They also struggle with more variable musical structures, such as those found in jazz or free improvisation as opposed to classical music, where the relationships between notes are less predictable. This is because partially due to LSTMs producing only a single output instead of a probability distribution over possible continuations. A solution to this latter limitation is to use a softmax layer at the output of the LSTM, which allows for a probability distribution over the possible next notes. However, this approach is limited to discrete possibilities, and so cannot capture the full range of possibilities, especially for many dimensional continuous musical parameters for instrument output.

\subsection{Mixture Density Recurrent Neural Networks}
Mixture Density Networks (MDNs) are a type of neural network which output a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), which is a weighted sum of multiple Gaussian distributions across any number of dimensions. This GMM is used as a probability distribution for the output note, as opposed to traditional networks, which output only a single output option~\citep{Bishop1994MDNs}. Mixture Density Recurrent Neural Networks (MDRNNs) are an RNN with a GMM output, and can solve the issue of RNN methods being seen as producing results which are too robotic or predictable over the long term to be truly human-like by allowing for the benefits of softmax outputs to be extended to continuous musical parameters. It also extends recurrent architectures in a way which allows them to better handle multiple continuous dimensions simultaneously, such as pitch, time, timbre, and many more, enabling more holistic prediction models.

MDRNNs incorporate a GMM output by outputting the necessary parameters vectors $\pi$, $\mu$, and $\sigma$, each of which the same dimension as a standard single output. The $\pi$ parameter is the weight of each Gaussian in the mixture, the $\mu$ parameter is the mean of each Gaussian, and the $\sigma$ parameter is the standard deviation of each Gaussian. The output of the MDRNN is then a sample from the GMM, which can be used as the next note in the sequence. Figure~\ref{fig:mdrnn} shows a simple MDRNN architecture for sequential note prediction. The output of the MDN are the inputs to a GMM, which is then used to sample the next note in the sequence.

\end{figure}

MDRNNs have been used in multiple human prediction domains, including generating handwriting~\cite{Graves2013Handwriting}, sketching~\cite{Ha2017Sketch}, and touchscreen performances~\citep{Martin2018RoboJam}. In all cases GMMs were used to generate more flexible outputs in continuous domains where the goal was to seem human-like, and performed better than softmax outputs. Hence, MDRNNs are a natural fit for music prediction systems, where this is also the goal, and have succeeded in this domain previously too~\citep{Martin2019IMPSY}. This is because MDRNNs can probabilistically express future likelihoods for continuous parameters such as timing, dynamics, timbre, and articulation; creating natural variations in expression that more closely resemble human musical composition.

However, the benefits MDRNNs to musical prediction tasks come with a caveat. While the addition of probabilistic outputs introduces useful variability, this approach often amounts to merely adding noise to otherwise deterministic predictions. This is in contrast to human improvisation, which introduces variations with intentional structural and semantic meaning rather than random deviations. In this sense, MDRNNs take the disadavntages of standard RNN outputs being too structured, and move too far in the opposite direction, instead producing outputs with arbitrary randomness. Ideally, a system would be able to balance these two extremes, producing outputs which are both structured and meaningful, but also varied and creative. This is exactly what our research aims to achieve, by replacing the final step of note generation in a standard MDRNN, random selection from the generated GMM, with an algorithm which meaningfully guides the selection process.


\section{Heuristic-Based AI in Music}
Heuristic methods serve as an alternative to machine learning based approaches for AI music generation and prediction. While neural networks excel at capturing the statistical patterns in music, they often lack the structured knowledge that can be encoded through rules and search algorithms. Heuristic approaches provide a framework that can enforce musical constraints, guide exploration of the solution space, and incorporate domain knowledge that may be difficult to learn from data alone. This section examines two approaches to heuristic-based AI in music: rule-based systems, and search algorithms. The search algorithms subsection focuses on hybrid approaches that combine heuristics with machine learning models.
\subsection{Rules Based Systems}
Rule-based approaches have been used in algorithmic composition since the 1950s~\citep{Hiller1959ILLIAC}, providing well-defined frameworks for generating music according to hardcoded constraints. These systems typically encode music theory knowledge, stylistic conventions, and compositional techniques into these constraints, which then guide note selection and musical structure. This allows for the generation of music that is very structured and coherent, at the cost of creativity, flexibility, and adaptability to novel musical contexts.

The earliest known example of a computer generated composition was the ILLIAC Suite~\citep{Hiller1959ILLIAC}, which, while technically a markov model based system, used the markov model only for adding randomness to an otherwise completely rule based system. Another technique for rule-based composition is the use of grammars, which define a set of rules for recursively generating musical structures.~\cite{Steedman1984JazzGrammar} used such a grammar to generate jazz chord progressions, demonstrating how formal grammars can capture the underlying structure of musical styles. Rules based systems like these almost always focused on a single style, and were the most prevalent method of computer music generation in its early years~\citep{Moorer1972ComputerMusic}.

Sometimes rule-based systems utilise constraint satisfaction programming (CSP), a hybrid between rule-based and search-based systems for very complex sets of musical rules, where the only objective of search is to find a solution whih adheres to rules, which are encoded as constraints in a CSP algorithm. This is a declarative method, where only the rules are defined and then the system itself decides how to apply them. This allows CSPs to find solutions where multiple competing constraints, such as voice leading and harmonisation, exist. An example of this approach is the CHORAL system, intended to harmonise four-part chorales in the style of Bach by combining a set of over 270 musical rules~\citep{Ebicoglu1988CHORAL}.~\cite{Anders2011ConstraintSurvey} survey extensive research into both the creation of useful constraints for music, and their use in general systems, most of which employ CSPs.

In the context of NIMEs, rule-based systems offer several advantages: they provide predictable, theory-compliant outputs; they can incorporate domain-specific knowledge about particular instruments or styles; and they can generate results in real-time with computation time dependant only on the complexity of the ruleset. However, pure rule-based systems face significant limitations. They struggle with the flexibility and nuance of human performance, often producing robotic results that lack the subtle variations of true expressive composition. Additionally, the manual encoding of musical knowledge becomes infeasible when attempting to capture the totality of musical composition across musical styles, and instead extensive hand-crafting of separate rule sets is usually required for different genres or instruments. This leads to a lack of adaptability to novel musical contexts not specifically encoded into the rule system.

\subsection{Search Based Systems}
Search algorithms combined with musical heuristics enable more sophisticated composition by exploring possible continuations and selecting optimal musical paths according to defined criteria. These approaches view music generation as a search problem, where the varying array of possible musical continuations is an important component of composition, and pleasing musical composition comes from exploring the space of possible continuations and selecting the most promising ones through the use of effective heuristics.

The use of tree search algorithms as the sole process of music generation is rare, as typically either constraint based search methods such as CSPs will be used, or search algorithms will be used in combination with machine learning models. Hence, the discussed papers all combine tree search with some form of machine learning architecture. Such hybrid approaches represent a promising direction for NIMEs, leveraging the pattern-recognition capabilities of neural networks while enforcing structural coherence through rules and search strategies. These systems typically employ neural networks for generating basic musical material but constrain or guide these generations using rule-based systems. Our research is a hybrid approach of this type, and so research into these systems is particularly relevant to our work.

~\cite{Fu2023MCTS} developed one of the few examples of a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) based musical prediction system, using a transformer model as a successor function to explore the space of possible continuations for a monophonic instrument, and selecting the most promising ones using heuristics founded on rule-based methods. This approach allows for the generation of music that is both structured and creative, as it can explore a wide range of possibilities while still adhering to musical constraints. The results showed high coherence, and better accuracy at making musical predictions than either purely rule-based or purely machine learning based methods. This demonstrates the potential of our research, as we are also aiming to utilise MCTS to increase the coherence and predictive accuracy of a machine learning based system. While this paper is a promising example of the use of MCTS for guiding music generation, it doesn't include another key aspect of our research, which is the detection and continuation of key musical parameters in a piece through MCTS guidance. However, this use case of MCTS in music generation has been explored by~\cite{Ferreira2022LmMcts}, who used MCTS to steer a neural language model toward a particular desired emotion, and showed very promising results compared to other methods in human listening trials. Despite its specificity to a single musical parameter, this work demonstrates the potential of MCTS for guiding the generation of music toward specific goals, and in combination with the research of~\cite{Fu2023MCTS}, provides promising background for the effectiveness of techniques used in this thesis.

The effectiveness of hybrid approaches underscores a key insight for NIME development: rather than viewing heuristic-based and learning-based methods as competing approaches, they can be integrated to create systems that leverage the strengths of both. However, there is a gap in the application of hybrid approaches, as research into the guiding MDRNN based search using MCTS with heuristics based on desired musical parameters does not exist, despite all three components having been tested with positive results in the literature~\citep{Martin2019IMPSY, Fu2023MCTS, Ferreira2022LmMcts}. This is the gap that our research aims to fill, as MDRNNs could benefit from the structured guidance of heuristic search algorithms similarly to how other approaches have in this related research.

\section{Tree Search Algorithms}
Our research focuses on the ability of tree search algorithms, in particular Continuous Monte Carlo Tree Search (C-MCTS), to guide the selection process from a GMM output by an MDRNN in an interactive musical prediction system. This requires an understanding of C-MCTS, and the algorithms it is built upon, Beam Search and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS). This section will provide a theoretical overview of these algorithms, and some of their early applications in fields outside of music, given that their use within music is limited, to provide the background necessary for understanding the technical aspects of this thesis. It will also provide a brief overview of why they are applicable to our research goals.

\subsection{Beam Search}
Beam Search offers a computationally efficient alternative to more exhaustive search methods by maintaining only the most promising partial solutions at each step of sequence generation. It can be thought of as a compromise between greedy search, which selects only the current most optimal candidate at each step, and exhaustive search methods such as Breadth First Search (BFS), which explore all possible branches to a given depth. This is because beam search retains the top-$k$ candidate sequences based on cumulative heuristic scores at each step, extending greedy search while maintaining computational efficiency~\citep{Bisiani1992BeamSearch}.

The algorithm operates by expanding all possible next steps from the current set of partial sequences, scoring each expanded sequence using heuristic evaluations, then pruning all but the $k$-highest scoring sequences. This process continues iteratively until complete sequences are generated or a termination condition is met.

An early use of beam search was the Harpy system created by~\cite{Lowerre1976Harpy} for speech recognition, which maintained a set of hypotheses for possible phonetic sequences in a tree structure, and kept to a limited number of hypotheses by using beam search. Beam search continued to be used in speech recognition systems for many years, proving its effectiveness in this domain~\cite{Steinbiss1994BeamNLP}. A similar method of multiple hypothesis maintainence was used effectively by~\cite{Sutskever2014Seq2Seq} for sequence-to-sequence tasks, such as language translation, showing how a combination of beam search and neural networks can be effective.

The primary advantages of Beam Search include its relative computational efficiency and straightforward implementation. However, its limitations become apparent in generation tasks requiring exploration of diverse possibilities or long-term planning. Because Beam Search maintains only a limited set of candidates based on immediate scores, it can suffer from horizon effects where initially promising paths lead to suboptimal long-term outcomes, similarly to greedy search. In short, it mixes the advantages and disadvantages of greedy and exhaustive search methods.

\subsection{Monte Carlo Tree Search}
Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) addresses the limitations of Beam Search by balancing exploration and exploitation through a combination of tree search and random sampling. MCTS constructs a search tree incrementally through repeated simulation, building confidence in promising paths over time through selection, expansion, simulation, and backpropagation. This process is explained below, and is also shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mcts}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Selection}: Starting from the root node, the algorithm traverses the existing tree by selecting child nodes according to a tree policy, typically using the Upper Confidence Bound for Trees (UCT) formula~\citep{Kocsis2006UCT} (TODO: maybe change this formula to use expected reward + c times sqrt formula if that is what we use for system design, could be so if we use probability as expected reward):

\begin{equation}
\text{UCT}(n) = \frac{Q(n)}{N(n)} + c \cdot \sqrt{\frac{\ln(N(p))}{N(n)}}
\end{equation}

where $Q(n)$ is the accumulated reward at node $n$, $N(n)$ is the visit count, $N(p)$ is the parent's visit count, and $c$ is an exploration constant. The UCT formula balances exploitation (favoring nodes with high rewards) and exploration (favoring nodes with low visit counts), and this balance can be tweaked by adjusting the exploration constant.

\item \textbf{Expansion}: Upon reaching a leaf node, the tree is expanded by adding one or more child nodes representing possible next actions.

\item \textbf{Simulation}: From the newly added node(s), a simulation is conducted to estimate the value of the node, typically by sampling actions according to a simple default policy involving some degree of randomness until reaching a terminal state. In simulations involving very large depth, the simulation can be cut off at a certain depth and the value of the node can be estimated by a heuristic.

\item \textbf{Backpropagation}: The simulation result is propagated back up the tree, updating visit counts and heuristic values for all nodes along the path.
\end{enumerate}

While monte carlo methods, where random sampling is used to simultate outcomes to deterministic problems, were first developed by~\cite{Metropolis1949MonteCarlo} in the 1940s and have been used in search algorithms for many years~\cite{Brugmann1993MonteCarloGo}, the first use of MCTS in its modern form was by~\cite{Coulom2006MCTS}, who used it to develop Crazy Stone, a Go playing AI. The UCT selection formula was also only developed in 2006~\citep{Kocsis2006UCT}, and so it is only from this point that the effectiveness of MCTS has become widely recognised.~\cite{Coulom2006MCTS} demonstrated that MCTS could significantly outperform MiniMax search in games with large branching factors, such as Go, by effectively balancing exploration and exploitation. The algorithm's ability to learn from random simulations allowed it to adaptively focus on promising regions of the search space, leading to improved performance in complex decision-making tasks. MCTS was very quickly adopted into a variety of domains, including game playing, sample-based planning, and optimization problems, where these vast search spaces are common~\citep{Browne2012MCTSSurvey}. More recently, MCTS has also seen a lot of success when combined with deep learning, and such hybrid cases will be discussed in the next section.

A significant advantage of MCTS over Beam Search is its ability to balance exploration of the search space with exploitation of promising paths in a more natural way. Instead of maintaining a fixed set of candidates, MCTS adapts its search strategy to focus more on promising regions of the search space as it accumulates more information. Through repeated simulation, MCTS gradually builds confidence about the value of different choices, enabling more sophisticated long-term planning. However, standard MCTS implementations face challenges in continuous domains, where the state and action spaces cannot be easily discretized.

Continuous Monte Carlo Tree Search (C-MCTS) extends traditional MCTS to handle continuous state and action spaces, making it particularly suitable for exploring continuous domains, such as GMM outputs we aim to use it to explore in this thesis. Several approaches have been developed to handle continuous spaces in MCTS~\citep{Lei2021KBTree}, but we will focus on the most relevant to our research.

Progressive Widening addresses the challenge of infinite branching factors in continuous spaces by gradually increasing the number of child nodes as the parent node receives more visits. Formally, if a node has been visited $n$ times, the number of allowed children is limited to $kn^{\alpha}$, where $k$ and $\alpha$ are tuning parameters. When new children are not permitted, standard UCT selection is used to select a child node. This allows for promising nodes to be explored sufficiently before adding new children. When a new child node can be generated, it can either be randomly sampled from the probability distribution of the action space, for example the GMM output of an MDRNN in our case, or can use a probability distribution influenced by potential, where potential can be estimate using kernal-based methods.

Kernel-based methods use kernel functions to generalize rewards across similar states and actions in continuous spaces. Rather than treating each continuous value as entirely distinct, kernel methods establish similarity metrics that allow information sharing between nearby points in the parameter space. This provides an advantage over standard progressive widening as it doesn't simply reduce continuous domains back down into discrete search, but instead maintains continuous domains with continuous heuristic values applied as a function across the continuous space of actions. However, this comes at the cost of increased computational complexity. Kernal Regression Upper Confidence Trees (KR-UCT) and their use in MCTS selection are discussed by~\cite{Lisy2016KRUCT}.

Baysian optimisation methods take the techniques of kernal-based methods a step further. Instead of only using a kernal function to smooth nearby values in parameter space, baysian optimisation can account for entire GMMs, providing estimated values for the entire continuous parameter space based on sampled values. This allows for a more accurate estimate of the value of a given action, but at the cost of additional computational complexity. It has been primarily implemented for solving partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs)~\citep{Mern2021BayesianMCTS}. Since our implementation of C-MCTS for structured MDRNN predictions utilises GMM outputs, and will only use limited sampling compared to many other MCTS problems, progressive widening with baysian optimisation is the most applicable method to our problem. \section{Applications of Combined Machine Learning and Search}
The integration of machine learning prediction models with search algorithms has emerged as a powerful AI method across multiple domains. By combining the pattern recognition capabilities of neural networks with the structured exploration of search algorithms, these hybrid approaches achieve results that surpass what either technique could accomplish independently. These examples demonstrate how the combination of prediction and search has been successful in other domains, providing valuable insights into how these successes could be applied to NIMEs.

\subsection{Game Playing AI}
Game playing systems have demonstrated perhaps the most obvious successes of combining neural evaluation with tree search methods, achieving superhuman performance in games once thought to be beyond computational reach. AlphaGo, developed by~\cite{Silver2016AlphaGo}, combined deep convolutional neural networks for position evaluation with Monte Carlo Tree Search for action selection to defeat Lee Sedol, considered one of the greatest ever Go players, in a five game match. This hybrid approach was further refined in AlphaGo Zero~\citep{Silver2017AlphaGoZero}, which learned solely through self-play, eliminated human knowledge entirely and demonstrating even stronger performance, and then AlphaZero~\citep{Silver2018AlphaZero}, which additionally boasted very strong results in both Chess and Shogi. Monte-carlo methods were later also used to develop Pluribus, the first superhuman level six-player texas-holdem poker AI~\citep{Brown2019Pluribus}. In these systems, MCTS was used primarily as a policy improvement operator, boosting the performance of an already strong neural network by looking deeply into the consequences of its predictions, or to learn from self-play. While we don't use MCTS for policy improvement in the same way, the goal of our research is still to use MCTS to improve the performance of a neural network, and so the success of these systems provides strong evidence for the potential effectiveness of this approach.

\subsection{Natural Language Processing}
Natural language processing systems have increasingly adopted hybrid approaches that combine the human imitation of neural language models with the structured guarantees provided by search and grammar constraints. Neural language models excel at capturing patterns in language, but may generate outputs that violate syntactic or semantic constraints. Search algorithms guided by these neural models can explore the space of possible outputs while ensuring adherence to formal rules. Beam search has become the main decoding strategy for neural machine translation and text generation systems ~\cite{Sutskever2014Seq2Seq}. In Google's Neural Machine Translation system, sophisticated beam search with length normalization and coverage penalties was a significant factor in the quality of their results~\citep{Wu2016GoogleNMT}.~\cite{Hokamp2017GridBeamSearch} extended this approach with grid beam search, which enforces lexical constraints while generating text. The success of hybrid NLP systems suggests that similar techniques could effectively balance learned patterns with formal structure in the musical domain.

Robot motion planning and autonomous driving systems face particularly stringent real-time constraints while requiring both the adaptability of learned models and the guarantees of structured search algorithms. For motion planning problems, regular search techniques such as A* cannot generate effective paths, as during the execution of the path the environment around the agent will be dynamically changing in an unpredictable way, which can affect the validity and optimality of generated paths. Hence, because the states after executing an action are non-deterministic, RNN are used to generate predicted nodes for each point in the search tree. These RNNs are good at accounting for the complex dynamic environment, and can provide reasonable predictions for what some realistic future scenarios could be after executing a set of actions. The agent can then search through these RNN created environmental prediction trees, often using MCTS, to find paths which more effectively account for the unpredictable environment. This is a similar process to the algorithm in our research.

One hybrid method used in this space is to use an RNN to convert a non-deterministic partially-observable problem into a more simple deterministic problem which can be solved by a standard planner.~\cite{Karkus2017QMDP} developed QMDP-net, which embeds planning within a neural architecture, integrating perception mechanisms with planning to allow for planning algorithms to better match the true environment. An inverse methodology is to reduce the action and state space to discrete actions for performing a search or planning algorithm, using a neural network at the end of simulation nodes to evaluate states. This approach is similar to AlphaGo~\citep{Silver2016AlphaGo}, and is used effectively for motion planning by~\cite{Weingertner2020MCTS}.

The most relevant approach to our work is where the neural network is used to generate action probability distributions, either of the agent itself, of other agents, or of the environment, and then MCTS is used to search through this generated action space. One example of this is the deep-MCTS algorithm by~\cite{Chen2020DeepMCTS}, which generated control commands for autonomous vehicles by using a combination of two deep neural networks and MCTS. The first neural network was the vehicle state prediction network, which predicted the future state of the vehicle given a current state and performed action. The second neural network was the action value prediction network, which for all possible actions provides action probabilities and expected rewards. This latter network guides the selection and expansion of nodes in the MCTS tree, which then searches predicted positive actions over multiple time steps to determine which action to take. While this approach is already very similar to our own, the KB-Tree algorithm by~\cite{Lei2021KBTree} is even more similar, as it uses an MDRNN to generate a GMM as an action probability distribution, and then uses C-MCTS with progressive widening, updated using both kernal regression and baysian optimisation, to search through the generated action space. KB-Tree outperformed the state-of-the-art in both reinforement learning and MCTS-based techniques in simulated parking tests.

The similarity of these mentioned approaches to our own research is notable, as while the domains are entirely different, the techniques used are very similar and the results are very promising. Given that these approaches are applicable to interactive musical prediction systems in a similar way, as this domain faces similar challenges to motion planning, we feel that the lack of research into applying these methods to musical prediction is a significant gap in the literature worth exploring. 

\section{IMPSY}
While our research pulls from a diverse range of background knowledge, as has been explored in previous subsections, it directly builds upon IMPSY, developed by~\cite{Martin2019IMPSY}. Hence, an understanding of IMPSY, including its design purpose, strengths, and weaknesses, its critical to understanding the motivation behind our research.

IMPSY is a real-time interactive NIME that uses an MDRNN based approach to generate musical predictions. It is designed for collaboration with human improvisation, and individual models for various instruments and performer styles can be trained quickly by feeding the training model data from human performer improvisation on the instrument of choice. Specifically, the model is trained to predict inputs to the instrument, not its outputs, meaning that it predicts the values of instrument controls rather than directly predicting their output. This provides the benefit of the model being agnostic to the mapping strategy from instrument controls to musical output. IMPSY also has multiple different collaborative modes, such as a battle mode where a human user and the MDRNN play simulataneously, but the interaction mode we will focus on within this thesis is the call and response mode. In this mode, the MDRNN is conditioned while the user plays, and begins to generate notes itself only when the user stops, letting the user take over again whenever they begin to use the instrument. The use of an MDRNN within IMPSY solves the problem traditional RNNs of only being able to output a single prediction. By outputting a probability distribution for next musical parameter values, randomness is added into predictions, reducing the overly rigid and robotic playing style of many traditional RNNs. However, this randomness isn't a perfect solution, as the randomness is arbitrary. Humans don't just add pure gaussian randomness when improvising, such as the type that MDRNNs generate. Instead they add controlled, guided, and meaningful randomness, which balances exploration of new ideas to create a unique performance, with exploitation of known musical concepts to ensure the performance is coherent and structured. 

This thesis is an attempt to more closely replicate this human improvisation process. While MDRNNs have achieved great progress in the field by combining the exploitative advantages of RNNs with the exploratory advantages of MDNs, these MDNs are unguided in their exploration. Research into other fields, particularly motion planning, has shown that guiding neural network predictions with search algorithms can lead to stronger outputs. Hence, we aim to combine the randomness of MDNs with the structure of heuristic-based search algorithms to create a system that can generate more expressive and coherent musical predictions. Figure~\ref{fig:mdrnn-vs-mcts} shows how the MDRNN-IMPSY prediction process compares to the MCTS-IMPSY prediction process. Note that the replacement of stochastic selection with a guided approach is closer to the human improvisational process, in that humans don't consider viable note options and select randomly. Notes are selected with reason, whether that reason is explicit or intuitive.
