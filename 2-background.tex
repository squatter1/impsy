We then discuss how machine learning has been used in music, and explore some different types of neural networks and their relevance to our report. This is followed by a look into symbolic AI, a different approach to machine learning based techniques, and its advantages and disadvantages in music generation compared to neural network based approaches. Finally, we briefly cover IMPSY, the system our research builds on, how it combines many computer music and machine learning techniques previously discussed to great effect, what its weaknesses and gaps in function are, and how applying combined machine learning and search techniques found in other fields can be applied in this context to address some of these weaknesses.

\section{New Interfaces for Musical Expression (NIMEs)}
\textit{The Cambridge Companion to Electronic Music} by~\cite{Collins2017Cambridge} describes three eras of music programming. The first era began with MUSIC-1, the first digital computer music synthesis program, which was followed be a series of versions collectively referred to as MUSIC-N. MUSIC-N introduced the concept of algorithmic composition, and is the precursor to modern computer music. The second era was defined by the introduction of real-time music programming languages, such as Max~\citep{Puckette2002Max} and Pure Data~\citep{Puckette1997PD}. Pure data is a graphical drag and drop modular programming language which allows for algorithmic compositions to be edited during performance, by editing the graphical coding of the system while a performance is in progress. While live performance with pure data is difficult and rudimentary compared to modern alternatives, it was a large step forward in the computer music space. The third and current era of music programming refers to the surge in more real-time and interactive musical interfaces, and a shift from programming being a tool to create musical algorithms, instruments, and performances, to programs becoming the instruments themselves. NIMEs can be defined as the core of what this shift represents, encompassing several concepts such as interactive musical prediction~\citep{MartinMDRNNinNIME2019}, generative accompaniment~\citep{Simon2008MarkovAccompany}, gestural interfaces~\citep{Takase2021CameraMelodyLSTM}, and much more. The term NIME originally comes from a conference series of the same name, which began in 2001 and now annually showcases the latest NIME related research.

The call and response mode of interactive musical prediction systems is the main focus area of our paper.

Gesture-based controllers map physical movements to musical parameters through a variety of sensing technologies. Early gestural controllers such as the Radio Baton by \cite{Mathews1991RadioBaton} established early frameworks for three-dimensional gestural control, which was later adapted into commercial technologies such as the Wii Remote \citep{Kiefer2008WiiInstrument} and Microsoft Kinect \citep{Silva2013KinectMusicalInterface}, making these interfaces both accesible and well known to the broader public. These adaptive systems allow performers to develop personalized control strategies that evolve through interaction rather than being predetermined by designers. Our research aims to be controller agnostic, allowing for complex controllers such as gestural controllers to be used in the system, and for the system to adapt to the unique control strategies of each performer.
\subsection{Augmented Instruments}
Augmented traditional instruments represent another significant approach, and can add enhanced control capabilities to instruments, adapt their outputs, or both, without sacrificing their familiarity. Machover's Hyperinstruments project \citep{Machover1992Hyperinstruments} was an early work focusing on control capabilities by enhancing traditional instruments with sensors and digital processing capabilities. More recent examples include McPherson's Magnetic Resonator Piano \citep{McPherson2018AugmentedPiano}, which introduces electromagnetic actuation to create new sound possibilities while maintaining traditional piano technique, and augmented string instruments which capture bow gestures and finger positions to enable extended sonic capabilities \citep{Overholt2011AugmentedViolin}. Our research also aims to be instrument agnostic, as any instrument which can map its outputs to a set number of dimensions can be used to train a model for the system. 
\subsection{Collaborative Networked Instruments}
Collaborative networked instruments have extend the concept of NIMEs beyond individual performance, allowing multiple performers to interact simultaneously across physical and virtual spaces, and for AI and human performers to collaborate in simultaneous performance. These collaborative interfaces often employ novel synchronization strategies and communication protocols to maintain musical coherence across distributed performance environments \citep{Carôt2009NetworkedMusic}. Our research does not cover the same breadth as these collaborative systems, as it only focuses on collaboration between one human and one AI performer, and does not involve both performing simultaneously. \section{Machine Learning in Music}
Machine learning techniques expand the possibilities of NIMEs significantly by allowing sophisticated and human-like generative music. Unlike rule-based approaches which rely on hardcoding of musical knowledge, machine learning models can discover patterns and relationships directly from musical data, allowing for more adaptive musical interactions. A well-trained musical prediction model can anticipate likely continuations of musical phrases, suggest complementary harmonizations, or generate real-time accompaniments to human performance on a variety of instruments. NIMEs can also be generalisable to multiple models trainable by users, allowing for easy expansion to different instruments and performance styles. The creation of effective machine learning based NIMEs requires an ability to capture both local patterns and global structures inherent in music. They must also balance the advantages of purely exploitative strategies, which aim to consistently generate the most likely next note, with explorative ones, which aim for more unique and human-like results. Many different machine learning architectures have been explored in attempt to balance these objectives, and each offers their own advantages and disadvantages. This section examines four key approaches that have proven particularly valuable for musical prediction tasks.

\subsection{Markov Models}

Markov models have been widely used in algorithmic composition due to their ability to capture local musical patterns and transitions. These probabilistic models operate on the principle that future states depend only on the current state (or a finite history of states), making them particularly suitable for modeling sequential musical events where local context strongly influences prediction. They are incredibly efficient computationally, requiring only a transition matrix to represent the probabilities of moving between states. However, this simplicity comes at the cost of limited expressive power, as Markov models struggle to capture long-term dependencies and hierarchical structures present in music.

The most basic form of Markov model is a first-order Markov chain. These chains model transitions where the next state depends solely on the current state, represented by a simple transition probability matrix. While computationally efficient, they often produce musically simplistic results that lack long-term structure (TODO citations, maybe Ames, 1989). Higher-order Markov chains incorporate longer histories by conditioning predictions on sequences of previous states, enabling more coherent musical phrases and motifs. Higher order markov chains are typically used withing Variable-order Markov models (VOMMs), which adaptively determine the appropriate context length for prediction based on available training data. Approaches such as Prediction Suffix Trees (PSTs) and Factor Oracles efficiently represent variable-length patterns by creating compact tree structures of musical sequences (Assayag \& Dubnov, 2004). This adaptability allows VOMMs to capture both short patterns when data is sparse and longer sequences when substantial evidence exists, making them particularly valuable for interactive systems where response time and predictive accuracy must be balanced (Conklin \& Witten, 1995). This separation enables HMMs to model more abstract musical concepts such as harmonic function, phrase structure, or emotional characteristics (Raphael, 1999). In harmony generation, Markov models effectively capture common chord progressions and voice-leading practices within specific musical styles. Simon et al. (2008) demonstrated that style-specific transition matrices can generate convincing chord progressions for jazz standards. For melodic prediction, Markov-based systems like Pachet's Continuator (2002) enable real-time melodic continuation that respects stylistic constraints while maintaining musical coherence, making them valuable in interactive performance contexts.
Recent research has explored hybrid approaches combining Markov models with other techniques to address their limitations in capturing hierarchical structure. Wang and Dubnov (2015) integrated variable-order Markov models with information dynamics to better capture both local transitions and long-term dependencies in musical sequences, showing improved performance in predictive tasks compared to standalone Markov approaches. However, markov models are limited by their simplicity, as they can only capture local patterns and transitions, and struggle to model complex musical structures. This limitation has motivated the development of more sophisticated neural network architectures that can capture more complex and less well-defined aspects of music.

\subsection{Feed-Forward Neural Networks}
Feed-forward neural networks (FNNs) were one of the earliest popular applications of neural networks to musical composition, offering the core advantage of neural networks, the ability to learn complex mappings between musical features and outputs. Unlike Markov models, which function based on explicit transition probabilities, FNNs can discover non-linear relationships within musical data, which can capture more complex musical structures. This led to them in most cases being a step-up from markov models in the field. However, FNNs face limitations in handling sequential data, as they require fixed-size inputs and lack mechanisms for capturing long-term dependencies, which led to them being used as the foundation for the development of recurrent neural networks (RNNs) as a successor to FNNs which could handle these issues.

The name 'feedforward' refers to the one direction flow of information through the network, where each layer's output serves as the input to the next layer, without any loops or backward flowing feedback connections. In an example of an augmented instrument, the first of these layers is the input layer, which could encode the controls of the instrument, this is followed by many hidden layers which transform these controls in complex ways, and then finally an output layer which produces a sound\citep{Todd1988AComposition} [Todd, P. M., "A Sequential Network Design for Musical Applications"]. TODO insert and reference a diagram of an FNN here.

Early applications of FNNs demonstrated their ability to learn harmonic relationships and predict chord progressions\citep{Lewis1988CreationNetworks} [Lewis, J. P., "Creation by Refinement and the Problem of Algorithmic Music Composition"], establishing that neural approaches could effectively capture some aspects of musical structure without directly programming musical rules. They have also been used to harmonize melodies \citep{Hild1992HarmonizingNetworks} [Hild, H., Feulner, J., \& Menzel, W., "Harmonizing Melodies by Finding Suitable Accompaniments"] and to create mappings between musical control parameters and synthesis algorithms \citep{Fiebrink2011RealTimeRecognition} [Fiebrink, R., "Real-time Human Interaction with Supervised Learning Algorithms for Music Composition and Performance"].

However, FNNs face fundamental limitations when applied to sequential musical data. Their requirement of a fixed size input size means that musical sequences must be encoded into fixed length vectors, forcing dimensionality reduction that leads to the loss of temporal information \citep{Huang2016AChallenges} [Huang, A. \& Wu, R., "A Deep Learning Approach to Music Generation: Challenges and Directions"]. This presents a significant challenge for modeling musical structure, as FNNs stuggle to represent how musical features relate to each another across time. To attempt to model the variable length of musical phrases, FNNs require truncation or padding of sequences, which limit the effectiveness of the model \citep{Fernandez2013AiGenjam:Algorithms} [Fernández, J. D. \& Vico, F., "AiGenjam: An Algorithmic Art Composition System Based on Genetic Algorithms"].

Somewhat effective workaround were used to address these limitations, such as using a fixed context of previous notes as input for predicting the next note \citep{Mozer1991ConnectivistMemory} [Mozer, M., "Connectivist Music Composition Based on Melodic, Stylistic, and Psychophysical Constraints"]. Despite these limitations, FNNs established important foundations for the use of neural networks in NIME applications. They demonstrated that neural networks could learn musical relationships directly from data, including complex musical features which are difficult to encode with hardcoded rules. Their limitations in handling sequential data directly motivated the development of architectures specifically designed for temporal processing, such as recurrent neural networks, which have for the most part become their successors in musical applications \citep{Engel2020DDSP:Processing} [Engel, J., et al., "DDSP: Differentiable Digital Signal Processing"].
\subsection{Recurrent Neural Networks}
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) extend beyond the limitations of both Markov models and feedforward neural networks by maintaining internal states that can capture more complex temporal dependencies in musical sequences. Unlike FNNs, which process inputs independently with no inbuild mechanism for processing sequences, RNNs incorporate backward flowing feedback connections which allow information to persist across time steps \citep{Todd1989ConnectioMusic} [Todd, P. M., "A Connectionist Approach to Algorithmic Composition"]. \citep{Mozer1994NeuralGeneration} [Mozer, M., "Neural Network Music Composition by Prediction: Exploring the Benefits of Psychoacoustic Constraints and Multi-scale Processing"]. In addition, RNNs utilise parameter sharing, which allows them propogate learning weights within the network over multipl time steps, which allows them to not have to constantly relearn already determined musical rules which are likely to persist over time (Ian Goodfellow, Yoshua Bengio, and Aaron Courville. Deep Learning. MIT Press, 2016). However, RNNs can suffer from the vanishing gradient problem, where gradient information diminishes exponentially as it is propagated backward through time \citep{Bengio1994LearningDependencies} [Bengio, Y., et al., "Learning Long-Term Dependencies with Gradient Descent is Difficult"]. Or the exploding gradient problem, where the gradients grow exponentially as they are propagated backward through time \citep{Pascanu2013OnNetworks} [Pascanu, R., et al., "On the Difficulty of Training Recurrent Neural Networks"]. This limitation is particularly problematic for music, where structural relationships often span dozens or hundreds of time steps. In practice, this means basic RNNs often fail to capture important long-range dependencies that create coherent musical phrases and sections \citep{Hochreiter1998VanishingNetworks} [Hochreiter, S. \& Schmidhuber, J., "Vanishing Gradients in Recurrent Networks: Problem and Solutions"].

RNN architecture consists of a hidden state that acts as a form of memory, allowing information to persist across time steps. This hidden state is updated at each time step based on both new input and its previous value, making RNNs particularly suited for sequence modeling tasks like music generation \citep{Todd1989ConnectioMusic}. Weights are kept when the hidden state is updated, allowing the network to learn long-term dependencies in the data. TODO: diagram of RNNs here with more explanation.

Early applications of RNNs to music demonstrated their ability to learn both melodic and harmonic progressions and remember them \citep{Eck2002FirstNetwork}, representing a significant advancement over FNNs. RNNs have since been applied across diverse musical tasks, including melody generation \citep{Colombo2018ExpressiveNetworks}, harmonization \citep{Hadjeres2017DeepJS:Chorales}, and real-time accompaniment \citep{Franklin2006RecurrentAlgorithm}.

However, basic RNNs face significant challenges when modeling music. Most prominently, they suffer from the vanishing gradient problem during training, where gradient information diminishes exponentially as it is propagated backward through time \citep{Bengio1994LearningDependencies}. This limitation is particularly problematic for music, where structural relationships often span dozens or hundreds of time steps. In practice, this means basic RNNs often fail to capture important long-range dependencies that create coherent musical phrases and sections \citep{Hochreiter1998VanishingNetworks}. The exploding gradient problem, where the gradients grow exponentially as they are propagated backward through time, can also occur \citep{Pascanu2013OnNetworks}. These challenges make it difficult for basic RNNs to learn complex musical structures that span entire compositions, limiting their effectiveness in generative music tasks.

Real-time musical applications introduce additional constraints. While RNNs can theoretically process sequences of arbitrary length, practical implementations require careful optimization to maintain low latency responses necessary for interactive performance settings \citep{Boulanger-Lewandowski2012ModelingNetworks}. Various approaches to mitigate these challenges include architectural modifications like time-windowing, where prediction is based on a limited temporal context to maintain computational efficiency \citep{Briot2017DeepSurvey}.

Despite these limitations, RNNs established the foundation for neural sequence modeling in music generation systems and demonstrated the potential for learning-based approaches to capture musical relationships across multiple time scales. Their architectural limitations ultimately motivated the development of more sophisticated recurrent architectures specifically designed to address the challenges of long-term dependency modeling \citep{Eck2002LearningLSTMs}.

\subsection{Long Short Term Memory Networks}
Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTMs) are a type of RNN which address the vanishing and exploding gradient problems of standard RNNs, enabling more effective learning of long-term dependencies crucial for musical coherence. Their gated architecture provides selective memory capabilities that better align with musical structures spanning multiple time scales. LSTMs can remember critical information over extended periods, but also forget irrelevant information, keeping them computationally efficient and effective for music generation tasks whilst enhancing the advantages of RNNs. Most of the time that RNNs are mentioned in other sections of this report, they are referring to LSTMs, as these are the most commonly used RNNs in music generation tasks today (TODO: cite a survey paper?).

The LSTM architecture introduces three specialized gates; the input, forget, and output gates; which regulate information flow through the network \citep{Hochreiter1997LongMemory}. These gates determine which information should be added to the cell state, which should be removed, and which should be output, allowing the network to selectively preserve important musical features while discarding irrelevant ones. This selective memory mechanism is particularly valuable for music, where relationships between notes can span varying temporal distances depending on the musical context \citep{Eck2002LearningLSTMs}. In music generation, LSTMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities. Unlike standard RNNs, they can maintain coherent harmonic progressions across extended musical phrases while simultaneously managing multiple independent voices \citep{Boulanger-Lewandowski2012ModelingNetworks}. Performance systems like DeepBach \citep{Hadjeres2017DeepJS:Chorales} and BachBot \citep{Liang2017AutomaticLSTMs} have demonstrated that LSTMs can capture sophisticated stylistic elements of musical composition, producing outputs that can sometimes fool expert listeners in musical Turing tests.

Real-time LSTM prediction systems have shown promise in interactive musical applications. Performance RNN \citep{Simon2018PerformanceTransformer} demonstrated that LSTMs can learn expressive timing and dynamics directly from performance data, producing musical outputs with human-like expressivity. Similarly, the Folk-RNN system \citep{Sturm2016MusicNetworks} showed that LSTMs could capture stylistic patterns specific to traditional musical genres. These applications highlight the LSTM's ability to balance predictive accuracy with stylistic coherence, making them suitable for responsive musical interfaces.

Training considerations for musical LSTMs remain significant challenges. Because musical data often contains relationships at multiple hierarchical levels; eg on the levels of beats, phrases, and sections; LSTMs require careful architectural design and training strategies. Approaches like hierarchical LSTMs \citep{Roberts2018HierarchicalAudio} have been developed to model multiple temporal scales simultaneously. Additionally, techniques like scheduled sampling \citep{Bengio2015ScheduledNetworks} and curriculum learning \citep{Jaques2017TuningSampling} have been employed to improve training stability and generalization.
Despite their advantages over standard RNNs, LSTMs still face limitations in capturing very long-range dependencies that span entire musical compositions \citep{Huang2018MusicTransformer}. They also struggle with maintaining global coherence while simultaneously preserving local detail—a fundamental tension in generative music systems. In addition, standard LSTMs cannot handle the inherent uncertainty and creative variability present in musical performance, as they produce a single output instead of a probability distribution over possible continuations \citep{Sturm2019MachineLimitations}.

A solution to the limitation of not allowing probabilistic outputs is to use a softmax layer at the output of the LSTM, which allows for a probability distribution over the possible next notes \citep{Graves2013GeneratingNetworks}. However, this approach is limited to discrete possibilities, and so cannot capture the full range of possibilities, especially for many dimensional continuous musical parameters for instrument output.

LSTMs have become fundamental to music generation due to their benefits for long-term musical structure, polyphonic music generation, and real-time prediction. Despite their added complexity over standard RNNs, LSTMs offer significant advantages for capturing the complex temporal relationships inherent in music, making them valuable for interactive musical systems. However, the deterministic nature of standard LSTM outputs can lead to overly predictable generations that lack the creative variations characteristic of human performance \citep{Sturm2019MachineLimitations}, and even when non-determinism is added to outputs with softmax, it can only handle discrete possibilities. The majority of machine learning systems aiming to imitate human like performance and musical composition choose at each step the most probable next note, which can lead to overly predictable and uninteresting music.

\subsection{Mixture Density Recurrent Neural Networks}
Mixture Density Recurrent Neural Networks (MDRNNs) allow for RNNs to output a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), which is a weighted sum of multiple Gaussian distributions across any number of dimensions. This GMM is used as a probability distribution for the output note, as opposed to traditional RNNs, which output only a single output option. This solves the issue of RNN methods being seen as producing results which are too robotic or predictable over the long term to be truly human-like, and allows for the benefits of softmax outputs to be extended to continuous musical parameters. It also extends recurrent architectures in a way which allows them to handle multiple continuous dimensions simultaneously, such as pitch, time, timbre, and many more, enabling more holistic prediction models.

MDRNNs incorporate a GMM output by making their output parameters $\pi$, $\mu$, and $\sigma$, each of which the same dimension as a standard single output, which are the coefficients of the GMM. The $\pi$ parameter is the weight of each Gaussian in the mixture, the $\mu$ parameter is the mean of each Gaussian, and the $\sigma$ parameter is the standard deviation of each Gaussian. The output of the MDRNN is then a sample from the GMM, which can be used as the next note in the sequence. In musical applications, MDRNNs offer significant advantages for modeling expressive parameters that exist on continuous scales. Unlike discrete note events, parameters such as timing, dynamics, timbre, and articulation often follow complex continuous distributions that cannot be adequately represented by single-point predictions \citep{Simon2017PerformanceNetworks}. By modeling these dimensions probabilistically, MDRNNs can generate performances with natural variations in expression that more closely resemble human musical composition \citep{Thickstun2019ModelingMDRNNs}.

The application of MDRNNs to interactive musical prediction systems, including complex controller mappings, has seen much success. Systems like PerformanceRNN with Conditioning \citep{Simon2018PerformanceTransformer} demonstrate how these models can predict continuous control parameters for synthesizers or digital instruments. Similarly, MDRNN architectures have been applied to generate realistic timing and dynamics in drum performances \citep{Makris2019RhythmicNetworks}, where capturing timing variations is essential to generating human-like groove. MDRNNs can also mirror the improvisational capabilities of human performers well by utilising a process closer to how humans think musically \citep{Roberts2018HierarchicalAudio}. This makes them particularly valuable for interactive systems where predictability must be balanced with novelty.

However, significant gaps remain in the application of MDRNNs to musical generation tasks. While the addition of probabilistic outputs introduces variability, this approach often amounts to merely adding noise to otherwise deterministic predictions \citep{Wang2019LimitationsGeneration}. This stands in contrast to human improvisation, which introduces variations with intentional structural and semantic meaning rather than random deviations \citep{Agres2016EvaluationMusic}. In this sense, MDRNNs take the disadavntages of standard RNN outputs being too structured, and move too far to the other extreme, instead producing outputs with arbitrary randomness. Ideally, a system would be able to balance these two extremes, producing outputs which are both structured and meaningful, but also varied and creative. This is exactly what our research aims to achieve, by creating an algorithm which guides the selection process from a generated GMM, instead of just selecting randomly.


\section{Heuristic-Based AI in Music}
The integration of heuristic methods with machine learning approaches offers powerful capabilities for music generation and interactive performance systems. While neural networks excel at capturing the statistical patterns in music, they often lack the structured knowledge that can be encoded through rules and search algorithms. Heuristic approaches provide a complementary framework that can enforce musical constraints, guide exploration of the solution space, and incorporate domain knowledge that may be difficult to learn from data alone. This section examines three approaches to heuristic-based AI in music: rule-based systems, search algorithms, and hybrid approaches that combine heuristics with machine learning models.
\subsection{Rules Based Systems}
Rule-based approaches have been used in algorithmic composition for decades, providing well-defined frameworks for generating music according to hardcoded constraints. These systems typically encode music theory knowledge, stylistic conventions, and compositional techniques into these constraints, which then guide note selection and musical structure. This allows for the generation of music that is very structured and coherent, at the cost of creativity, flexibility, and adaptability to novel musical contexts.

Traditional rule-based systems like EMI (Experiments in Musical Intelligence) leverage extensive music theory principles to analyze and recombine existing musical works into new compositions which maintain coherence (Cope, D. [Experiments in Musical Intelligence]). Other systems, such as Ebcioglu's CHORAL system, demonstrate how Bach's chorale harmonization techniques could be formalized into a rule-based algorithm(Ebcioglu, K. [An Expert System for Harmonizing Four-Part Chorales]). Hiller and Isaacson's ILLIAC suite proved that counterpoint rules could also be formalized into algorithms [Music Composed with Computers]. Rules based systems like these typically convert music theory concepts like voice leading, harmonic progression, and rhythmic patterns into well-defined rule sets. They are often based around classical music, as it is a genre where the many well defined rules exist, but they have also been used for other styles, such as for encoding harmonic progression rules in jazz standards (Steedman, M. [A Generative Grammar for Jazz Chord Sequences]).

In the context of NIMEs, rule-based systems offer several advantages: they provide predictable, theory-compliant outputs; they can incorporate domain-specific knowledge about particular instruments or styles; and they can generate results in real-time with computation time dependant only on the complexity of the ruleset. This enables them to be used for real-time musical interaction, such as Dannenberg's real-time accompaniment system which tracks a performer and applies stylistic rules to generate accompaniment [A Computer Music Programming Language].

However, pure rule-based systems face significant limitations. They struggle with the flexibility and nuance of human performance, often producing robotic results that lack the subtle variations of true expressive composition. Additionally, the manual encoding of musical knowledge becomes infeasible when attempting to capture the totality of musical composition across musical styles, and instead extensive hand-crafting of separate rule sets is usually required for different genres or instruments. This leads to a lack of adaptability to novel musical contexts not specifically encoded into the rule system [The Algorithmic Composer].
\subsection{Search Based Systems}
Search algorithms combined with musical heuristics enable more sophisticated composition by exploring possible continuations and selecting optimal musical paths according to defined criteria. These approaches approach music generation as a search problem, where the varying array of possible musical continuations is an important component of composition, and pleasing musical composition come from optimising the direction of composition through creation of a heuristic to rank musical sequences.

Constraint satisfaction programming (CSP) is one type of search which takes a lot of influence from rule-based approaches. A CSP system encodes musical rules as constraints, and then searches for solutions that satisfy all constraints. This differes from rule-based systems as it is a declarative process rather than a procedural one, meaning that instead of defining exactly how rules are applied, and in what hierarchy of precedence, the programmer instead simply defines the rules and then the system itself decides how to apply them. This allows CSPs to find solutions where multiple competing constraints, such as voice leading and harmonisation, exist. Anders and Miranda developed a CSP approach that combines harmonic rules with efficient search algorithms to generate chord progressions and counterpoint [Constraint Programming Systems for Modeling Music Theories]. Other search methods used include tree search algorithms, which explore multiple possible continuations of a musical sequence and evaluate branches based on musical metrics. These algorithms can be used to generate music that is both coherent and novel, by exploring the space of possible continuations and selecting the most promising ones. Assayag's OMax system implemented variable-depth search algorithms that could adapt their computational complexity based on available processing time [OMax Brothers: A Dynamic Topology of Agents for Improvisation Learning]. This system was used to generate music in real-time, and could adapt to the performer's input to create responsive musical accompaniment. More complex tree search approaches, such as beam search and Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) have also been used. Sturm and Ben-Tal employed MCTS to generate folk music continuations, balancing exploration of novel musical ideas with exploitation of learned patterns [Taking the Models back to Music Practice]. In interactive settings, these approaches allow for dynamic adjustment of search parameters based on performer input, creating responsive systems that adapt to changing musical contexts. However, these tree search methods are underexplored in NIMEs, and are more common in other fields, as will be discussed later in this chapter.

The limitations of search based methods include their computational cost, as they can require significant processing power to explore the space of possible musical continuations to a suitable depth and breadth. Pruning methods often remove many viable musical continuations, restricting how expressive the system can be. Additionally, search algorithms can struggle with the complexity of musical structure similarly to rule-based systems, although not quite to the same degree. This is because they usually operate in a discrete space, and involve heuristics which function on hardcoded rules rather than learned patterns, which can limit their ability to capture the full range of musical expression.
\subsection{Hybrid Approaches}
Hybrid approaches combining machine learning with heuristic-based approaches represent a promising direction for NIMEs, leveraging the pattern-recognition capabilities of neural networks while enforcing structural coherence through rules and search strategies. These systems typically employ neural networks for generating basic musical material but constrain or guide these generations using rule-based systems. Our research is a hybrid approach, as it uses a neural network to generate a GMM, but then uses heuristic search to guide the selection of the next note from the GMM.

Hadjeres and Nielsen's DeepBach model combines a neural network with constraints derived from Bach's compositional practice, producing convincing chorales while maintaining strict voice-leading rules [DeepBach: a Steerable Model for Bach Chorales Generation]. Similarly, Lattner and colleagues' C-RBM approach uses a constrained Restricted Boltzmann Machine to generate melodies that adhere to structural constraints [Imposing Higher-Level Structure in Polyphonic Music Generation].

In interactive performance contexts, hybrid systems can address the limitations of purely machine learning based and purely rule-based methods simultaneously. Herremans and Chew's MorpheuS system employs a constraint satisfaction approach to impose structural constraints on music generated by a neural network, creating coherent long-term structure [MorpheuS: Generating Structured Music with Constrained Patterns and Tension]. Roberts and colleagues demonstrated how introducing structural markers into sequence generation can improve the global coherence of neural network music generation [A Hierarchical Latent Vector Model for Learning Long-Term Structure in Music].

Training networks with heuristic constraints represents another promising hybrid approach. Rather than applying constraints post-generation, these methods incorporate musical rules directly into the training process. Eck and Schmidhuber pioneered this approach by training networks on chord sequences that follow common harmonic progressions [Finding Temporal Structure in Music: Blues Improvisation with LSTM Recurrent Networks]. More recently, Lattner and colleagues demonstrated that imposing structural constraints during training can lead to networks that internalize musical rules, producing more coherent outputs [Learning Musical Structure Through Self-Supervision].


The effectiveness of hybrid approaches underscores a key insight for NIME development: rather than viewing rule-based and learning-based methods as competing paradigms, they can be integrated to create systems that leverage the strengths of both approaches while mitigating their respective weaknesses. However, there is a gap in the application of hybrid approaches, as research into the combination of MDRNNs with heuristic search algorithms is limited. This is the gap that our research aims to fill, as MDRNNs could benefit from the structured guidance of heuristic search algorithms similarly to how other approaches have in this related research.

\section{Tree Search Algorithms}
Our research focuses on the ability of tree search algorithms, in particular Continuous Monte Carlo Tree Search (C-MCTS), to guide the selection process from a GMM output by an MDRNN in an interactive musical prediction system. This requires an understanding of C-MCTS, and the algorithms it is built upon, Beam Search and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS). However, their use in previous NIME research, while promising, has been limited. Hence, this section will provide a theoretical overview of these algorithms, and some of their early applications in fields outside of music, to provide the background necessary for understanding the technical aspects of this report. It will also provide a brief overview of why they are applicable to our research goals.

\subsection{Beam Search}
Beam Search offers a computationally efficient alternative to more exhaustive search methods by maintaining only the most promising partial solutions at each step of sequence generation. It can be thought of as a compromise between greedy search, which selects only the current most optimal candidate at each step, and exhaustive search methods such as Breadth First Search (BFS), which explores all possible branches to a given depth. This is because beam search retains the top-$k$ candidate sequences based on cumulative heuristic scores at each step, extending greedy search while maintaining its computational efficiency.

The algorithm operates by expanding all possible next steps from the current set of partial sequences, scoring each expanded sequence using heuristic evaluations, then pruning all but the $k$-highest scoring sequences. This process continues iteratively until complete sequences are generated or a termination condition is met. TODO: insert a diagram of beam search here with more explanation.

Beam Search was oroginally used for speech recognition systems by Lowerre, who described the algorithm's ability to efficiently navigate large search spaces while maintaining multiple hypotheses \cite{lowerre1976harpy}. The approach was further refined by Steinbiss et al. for natural language processing applications, establishing its value for sequence generation tasks \cite{steinbiss1994improvements}. Sutskever et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of Beam Search for neural machine translation, coupling it with sequence-to-sequence models to achieve state-of-the-art results \cite{sutskever2014sequence}.

The primary advantages of Beam Search include its relative computational efficiency and straightforward implementation. However, its limitations become apparent in generation tasks requiring exploration of diverse possibilities or long-term planning. Because Beam Search maintains only a limited set of candidates based on immediate scores, it can suffer from horizon effects where initially promising paths lead to suboptimal long-term outcomes. In short, it combines both the advantages and disadvantages of greedy and exhaustive search methods.

\subsection{Monte Carlo Tree Search}
Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) addresses the limitations of Beam Search by balancing exploration and exploitation through a combination of tree search and random sampling. Originally developed for perfect information games like Go, MCTS has been adapted to sequence generation tasks including creative composition.

MCTS constructs a search tree incrementally through repeated simulation, building confidence in promising paths over time through four key phases, with can also be seen in the (TODO: diagram below):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Selection}: Starting from the root node, the algorithm traverses the existing tree by selecting child nodes according to a tree policy, typically using the Upper Confidence Bound for Trees (UCT) formula:

\begin{equation}
\text{UCT}(n) = \frac{Q(n)}{N(n)} + c \cdot \sqrt{\frac{\ln(N(p))}{N(n)}}
\end{equation}

where $Q(n)$ is the accumulated reward at node $n$, $N(n)$ is the visit count, $N(p)$ is the parent's visit count, and $c$ is an exploration constant. The UCT formula balances exploitation (favoring nodes with high rewards) and exploration (favoring nodes with low visit counts), and this balance can be tweaked by adjusting the exploration constant.

\item \textbf{Expansion}: Upon reaching a leaf node, the tree is expanded by adding one or more child nodes representing possible next actions.

\item \textbf{Simulation}: From the newly added node(s), a simulation is conducted to estimate the value of the node, typically by sampling actions according to a simple default policy involving some degree of randomness until reaching a terminal state. In simulations involving very large depth, the simulation can be cut off at a certain depth and the value of the node can be estimated by a heuristic.

\item \textbf{Backpropagation}: The simulation result is propagated back up the tree, updating visit counts and heuristic values for all nodes along the path.
\end{enumerate}

The foundational work on MCTS was introduced by Kocsis and Szepesvári, who developed the UCT algorithm that revolutionized computer Go and other perfect information games \cite{kocsis2006bandit}. Browne et al. provided a comprehensive survey of MCTS methods and applications, cataloging its success across numerous domains \cite{browne2012survey}. Coulom's seminal paper established the effectiveness of MCTS for Go, demonstrating its ability to handle large branching factors more effectively than traditional minimax approaches \cite{coulom2006efficient}.

A significant advantage of MCTS over Beam Search is its ability to balance exploration of the search space with exploitation of promising paths in a more natural way. Instead of maintaining a fixed set of candidates, MCTS adapts its search strategy to focus more on promising regions of the search space as it accumulates more information. Through repeated simulation, MCTS gradually builds confidence about the value of different choices, enabling more sophisticated long-term planning. However, standard MCTS implementations face challenges in continuous domains, where the state and action spaces cannot be easily discretized.

Continuous Monte Carlo Tree Search (C-MCTS) extends traditional MCTS to handle continuous state and action spaces, making it particularly suitable for exploring continuous domains, such as a GMM. Several approaches have been developed to handle continuous spaces in MCTS:

Progressive Widening addresses the challenge of infinite branching factors in continuous spaces by gradually increasing the number of child nodes as the parent node receives more visits. Formally, if a node has been visited $n$ times, the number of allowed children is limited to $kn^{\alpha}$, where $k$ and $\alpha$ are tuning parameters. When selecting child nodes when new children are not permitted, the algorithm rounds its selection to the nearest existing child node. This approach was formalized by Couëtoux et al. and has been applied to various continuous control problems \cite{couetoux2011continuous}. TODO: diagram showing progressive widening.

Double Progressive Widening extends this concept to both action and state spaces, allowing for stochastic transitions in continuous domains. This approach, developed by Couëtoux et al. in their followup work, is particularly valuable for applications where outcomes may have inherent uncertainty \cite{couetoux2012double}.

Kernel-based methods use kernel functions to generalize rewards across similar states and actions in continuous spaces. Rather than treating each continuous value as entirely distinct, kernel methods establish similarity metrics that allow information sharing between nearby points in the parameter space. This provides an advantage over progressive widening as it doesn't simply reduce continuous domains back down into discrete search, but instead maintains continuous domains with continuous heuristic values applied as a function across the continuous space of actions. However, this comes at the cost of increased computational complexity. Mahendran et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of this approach in continuous control tasks \cite{mahendran2018deep}.

C-MCTS can serve as an effective technique for guiding continuous search in many domains. Silver et al. applied MCTS to complex decision spaces with continuous parameters \cite{silver2016mastering}, and Wang et al. formalized continuous variants of MCTS for robotic control applications \cite{wang2018improvisation}.

Our implementation of C-MCTS for structured MDRNN predictions employs progressive widening with a specialized heuristic function which evaluates a musical continuations against structural parameters, aiming to imitate the values a human-performer would most likely achieve. The algorithm maintains a continuously updated search tree during performance, allowing it to adapt to changing contexts while ensuring coherence and guided adherence to current musical structure. This approach provides a novel solution to the challenges of balancing structure and creativity in interactive music generation systems, offering a promising direction for future NIME research.

\section{Applications of Combined Machine Learning and Search}
The integration of machine learning prediction models with search algorithms has emerged as a powerful AI method across multiple domains. By combining the pattern recognition capabilities of neural networks with the structured exploration of search algorithms, these hybrid approaches achieve results that surpass what either technique could accomplish independently. These examples demonstrate how the combination of prediction and search has been successful in other domains, providing valuable insights into how these successes could be applied to NIMEs.

\subsection{Game Playing AI}
Game playing systems have demonstrated perhaps the most obvious successes of combining neural evaluation with tree search methods, achieving superhuman performance in games once thought to be beyond computational reach. AlphaGo, developed by Silver et al., represents a major milestone in artificial intelligence, combining deep convolutional neural networks for position evaluation with Monte Carlo Tree Search for action selection \cite{silver2016mastering} to defeat Lee Sedol, considered one of the greatest ever Go players, in a five game match (TODO citation). Before this match, Go was one of the last remaining popular complete information games where humans were dominant (TODO, citation?), due to its incredibly large search space. The neural networks, trained through both supervised learning and reinforcement learning, provided heuristic guidance that dramatically pruned the search space, allowing MCTS to explore deeply along promising paths rather than wasting computation on clearly suboptimal moves.

This hybrid approach was further refined in AlphaGo Zero and AlphaZero, which eliminated human knowledge entirely and learned solely through self-play, demonstrating even stronger performance \cite{silver2017mastering}. The key insight in these systems was the synergy between the neural network and search algorithm: the network guided search toward promising regions, while search results provided training data to improve the network's predictions. As explained by Silver et al.: "MCTS may be viewed as a policy improvement operator" \cite{silver2018general}.

Similar hybrid approaches have been successfully applied to other games with high branching factors, including chess(CITE stokfish), shogi(CITE), and poker(CITE). In each case, the neural network provides fast approximate evaluation that guides tree search, while MCTS, or another tree search method, provides the structured exploration necessary to overcome the limitations of pure pattern recognition.

While these game playing systems use a very different method of combining neural networks and search algorithms to our research, they provide a valuable example of how the two can be combined in a way that is greater than the sum of its parts.

\subsection{Natural Language Processing}
Natural language processing systems have increasingly adopted hybrid approaches that combine the human imitation of neural language models with the structured guarantees provided by search and grammar constraints. Neural language models excel at capturing patterns in language but may generate outputs that violate syntactic or semantic constraints. Search algorithms guided by these neural models can explore the space of possible outputs while ensuring adherence to formal rules.

Beam search has become the main decoding strategy for neural machine translation and text generation systems \cite{sutskever2014sequence}. Wu et al. demonstrated that sophisticated beam search with length normalization and coverage penalties significantly improved the quality of Google's Neural Machine Translation system \cite{wu2016google}. Hokamp and Liu extended this approach with grid beam search, which enforces lexical constraints while generating text, ensuring that specific terms appear in the output \cite{hokamp2017lexically}.

More complex approaches incorporate formal grammars into the generation process. Xiao et al. developed a technique for constrained decoding that ensures neural language models produce outputs conforming to context-free grammars \cite{xiao2016sequence}. Yin and Neubig created a neural architecture that models the generation of abstract syntax trees, improving the syntactic correctness of outputs \cite{yin2017syntactic}.

These approaches parallel our work in musical generation, where patterns captured by MDRNNs must be combined with structural musical constraints. The success of hybrid NLP systems suggests that similar techniques can effectively balance learned patterns with formal structure in the musical domain.

\subsection{Trajectory Rollouts with Multiple Hypotheses}
Trajectory prediction systems incorporate uncertainty by maintaining multiple hypotheses about future states, a technique particularly valuable in domains where decisions have long-term consequences and future states depend on complex, partially observable factors. These approaches share conceptual similarities with the techniques proposed in this paper for musical trajectory generation. Trajectory rollouts are commonly used in robotics, autonomous driving, and human-robot interaction, where the ability to predict multiple possible futures is essential for safe and effective decision-making.

Multiple-model approaches to trajectory prediction, as developed by Blackman and Popoli, maintain a set of possible trajectory hypotheses and update their probabilities based on new observations \cite{blackman1999design}. This technique has been enhanced with neural predictors by Alahi et al., who developed Social LSTM models that predict multiple possible trajectories for pedestrians in crowded spaces \cite{alahi2016social}.

Gupta et al. extended this concept with their Social GAN architecture, which generates diverse socially acceptable trajectories by combining adversarial training with a social pooling mechanism \cite{gupta2018social}. The system maintains multiple hypotheses about how trajectories might evolve, allowing it to capture the complexity of human movement patterns.

These multiple hypothesis approaches have been further enhanced by incorporating search algorithms that explore the space of possible trajectories. Schmerling et al. combined probabilistic neural predictions with tree search to generate realistic human-robot interaction trajectories \cite{schmerling2018multimodal}. Their approach uses neural networks to predict likely human responses to robot actions, while tree search explores possible interaction sequences to find optimal robot policies.

The parallels to musical generation are clear: like human movement, musical trajectories are multimodal and complex, with multiple valid continuations from any given state. By maintaining multiple hypotheses and using search to explore them, systems can generate musical content that is both unique and coherent.

\subsection{Robot Motion Planning}
Robot motion planning and autonomous driving systems face particularly stringent real-time constraints while requiring both the adaptability of learned models and the guarantees of structured search algorithms. Most motion planning systems extend upon the work of trajectory rollouts with multiple hypotheses, combining neural prediction with search to generate safe and effective robot trajectories, while also requiring real-time decisions about which path to take in a complex environment.

For motion planning problems, regular search techniques such as A* cannot generate effective paths, as during the execution of the path the environment around the agent will be dynamically changing in a complex multi-dimensional way, which can unpredictably affect the validity and optimality of generated paths. Hence, because the states after executing an action are non-deterministic, RNNs are used to generate predicted nodes for each point in the search tree. These RNNs are good at accounting for the complex dynamic environment, and can provide reasonable predictions for what some realistic future scenarios could be after executing a set of actions. The agent can then search through these RNN created environmental prediction trees, often using MCTS, to find paths which more effectively account for the unpredictable environment. This is a similar process to the algorithm in our research.

Model predictive control enhanced with neural networks has emerged as a powerful technique for robotic control in complex environments. Williams et al. developed Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control, which uses neural dynamics models to predict future states while applying sampling-based optimization to find control trajectories that minimize cost \cite{williams2017model}.

In autonomous driving, hybrid approaches have proven particularly effective. Karkus et al. developed QMDP-net, which embeds differentiable planning within a neural architecture, allowing end-to-end learning of integrated perception and planning systems \cite{karkus2017qmdp}. Ichter et al. combined learned sampling distributions with traditional motion planning algorithms to efficiently navigate complex environments \cite{ichter2018learning}. Their approach uses neural networks to focus sampling on promising regions of the configuration space, while deterministic planning algorithms ensure path feasibility and safety.

Particularly relevant to our work is the application of Monte Carlo Tree Search to robotic control with continuous action spaces. Ueno et al. applied C-MCTS with progressive widening to real-time robot motion planning, demonstrating its effectiveness in continuous domains with tight computational constraints \cite{ueno2016application}. Chua et al. further extended this approach with their PETS algorithm, which combines probabilistic neural dynamics models with model predictive control and tree search planning \cite{chua2018deep}.

These robotic applications demonstrate the effectiveness of combining neural prediction with structured search in domains with continuous action spaces and real-time constraints. The success of progressive widening techniques for C-MCTS in robotics applications provides strong evidence for their potential effectiveness in continuous musical generation tasks, where similar constraints apply.

\section{IMPSY}
While our research pulls from all of this diverse background knowledge, it directly builds on the foundation of interactive musical prediction systems in NIMEs, specifically expanding upon IMPSY, developed by~\cite{MartinMDRNNinNIME2019}. Hence, an understanding of IMPSY, including its design purpose, strengths, and weaknesses, its critical to understanding the motivation behind our research.

IMPSY is a real-time interactive NIME that uses an MDRNN based approach to generate musical predictions. It is designed for collaraboration with human improvisation, and individual models for various instruments and performer styles can be trained quickly by feeding the training model data from human performer improvisation on the instrument of choice. IMPSY also has multiple different collaborative modes, such as a battle mode where a human user and the MDRNN play simulataneously, but the interaction mode we will focus on within this paper is the call and response mode. In this mode, the MDRNN is conditioned while the user plays, and begins to generate notes itself only when the user stops, letting the user take over again whenever they begin to use the instrument. Similarly to examples listed previously, the MDRNN layer within IMPSY solves the problem with traditional RNNs of only being able to output a single prediction. By outputting a probability distribution for next musical parameter values, randomness is added into predictions, reducing the overly rigid and robotic playing style of many traditional RNNs. However, this randomness isn't a perfect solution, as the randomness is arbitrary. Humans don't just add pure gaussian randomness when improvising, such as the type that MDRNNs generate. Instead they add controlled, guided, and meaningful randomness, which balances exploration of new ideas to create a unique performance, with exploitation of known musical concepts to ensure the performance is coherent and structured. 

Our paper is an attempt to more closely replicate this human improvisation process, while MDRNNs have achieved great progress in the field by combining the exploitative advantages of RNNs with the exploratory advantages of MDNs, these MDNs are unguided in their exploration. We aim to combine the randomness of MDNs with the structure of heuristic-based search algorithms, to create a system that can generate more expressive and coherent musical accompaniments.
